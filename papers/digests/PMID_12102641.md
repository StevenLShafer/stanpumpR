# McAuley 2002: epilepsy in women of reproductive age, PK review

## Study
This is a narrative review with no new data and no systematic search method. It covers the first-generation
AEDs (phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone, carbamazepine, ethosuximide, valproic acid) and the second-generation
AEDs (felbamate, gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine, tiagabine, topiramate, vigabatrin,
zonisamide). Topics:
- catamenial epilepsy (section 2);
- bone and lipid effects (section 3);
- interactions with oral contraceptives (section 4);
- pregnancy PK and TDM (section 5);
- teratogenicity, folate and vitamin K (section 6);
- breastfeeding (section 7);
- guidelines (section 8).

**Oral contraceptives (Table III, p8, rendered):**
- Induce CYP3A4, with lower OC exposure: carbamazepine, felbamate, oxcarbazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin and
  topiramate.
- Do not: ethosuximide (no OC study available), gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, tiagabine, valproic acid,
  vigabatrin and zonisamide (no OC study).
- The text (p8) states that valproic acid and ethosuximide are not enzyme inducers. It calls topiramate,
  felbamate and oxcarbazepine weak, CYP3A4-only inducers.
- Recommendation (p9): with an inducing AED, an OC containing 50 μg of estrogen or an extra method.

**Pregnancy (Table IV, p10, rendered):**
- Ethosuximide is listed as eliminated hepatically and renally, isozyme "Not known", with total and unbound
  concentrations each down by 50%.
- Plasma volume rises 40-50% (p9).
- Dose changes should follow clinical need, not total levels, except unbound phenytoin (p11). In 42 pregnant
  women on first-generation AEDs, 49% needed no change, 38% an increase and 6% a decrease (p11).

**Breastfeeding (Table VII, p16, rendered):** ethosuximide 0% bound, M/P 0.77-1.0, infants reach therapeutic
concentrations. The text (p16) says ethosuximide, primidone and phenobarbital are the first-generation AEDs
that give measurable infant concentrations.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
None. There are no concentration-effect data, and no MEAC, ke0 or potency ratios. It is not an opioid review.

## Covariates
- Pregnancy, through falling albumin and rising CYP2C9/2C19 activity and renal function (p10).
- Enzyme-inducing co-medication, as a perpetrator on oral contraceptives.
- No equations.

## Caveats
- **Nothing on ethosuximide as a victim.** The review has no data on inducer effects on ethosuximide clearance,
  and none on a valproate→ethosuximide interaction. Every ethosuximide mention was checked (pp3, 8, 10, 16 and
  reference 117).
- The pregnancy changes for ethosuximide rest on two references, one a single-patient case report (ref 117).
- Classifications reflect 2002 knowledge. Ethosuximide's metabolising isozyme is given as "Not known".
- Table IV prints "CYPC8/9" for carbamazepine (p10, rendered), a source typo for CYP2C8/9.
