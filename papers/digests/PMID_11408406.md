# Peyton 2001a: V/Q inhomogeneity increases gas uptake (two-compartment theory)

## Study
Theoretical computer study with no patients and no drug dosing. It is a steady-state, two-alveolar-compartment gas-exchange model with mass balance for each gas (PDF p.1-2).

- Ventilation and blood flow are split between two compartments. Inhomogeneity is set by the fraction of ventilation (or blood flow) given to compartment 1, from 0 (shunt) to 1.0 (dead space), with the other variable held even.
- Overall expired alveolar ventilation (VAE) is 4.1 L/min and Q is 4.8 L/min in all scenarios (p.4).
- O2 and CO2 content use the Kelman dissociation routines. The text gives no number for barometric pressure.

**Hypothetical gases study (p.4-7).** G1 and G2 have a blood-gas partition coefficient of 1.0.

- Scenario 1a: inspired fraction 0.5 each, mixed venous partial pressure (Pv) 340 Torr each, uptake 100 ml/min each. Inhomogeneity has no effect on uptake (neutral point).
- Scenario 1b: Pv of G1 is raised 20 Torr and Pv of G2 lowered 20 Torr. Combined uptake is unchanged. Uptake of the lower-uptake gas, G1 (threshold 100 ml/min), increases with inhomogeneity. The authors call this "paradoxical augmentation".
- Scenario 1c: Pv of G2 is 170 Torr and Pv of G1 is 510 Torr. Absolute exchange of both gases falls. G1 is being eliminated, and if elimination is read as negative uptake, its exchange still rises.
- Scenarios 1d and 1e: different inspired fractions (0.75 and 0.25) move the neutral point. With the shifted Pv, the threshold for G1 becomes 150 ml/min.
- Scenarios 2a and 2b: with the uptake of G2 fixed at 200 ml/min, paradoxical augmentation of G1 occurs and combined uptake rises. With G2 fixed at 50 ml/min, augmentation is absent and inhomogeneity lowers G1 uptake.

**Physiological gases study (p.4, 7).** The inspired mixture is 30% O2 and 70% N2O (Ostwald solubility 0.47).

- Pv of N2O is 468 Torr, giving N2O uptake of 100 ml/min in the homogeneous lung. This is consistent with the Severinghaus relation, N2O uptake = 1000/sqrt(t) ml/min with t in minutes, quoted by the authors.
- Scenario 3 holds Pv of O2 and CO2 constant. Scenario 4 holds O2 and CO2 uptake at 250 and 200 ml/min (derived: respiratory quotient 0.8).
- Paradoxical augmentation of N2O uptake is predicted in both scenarios, whether inhomogeneity comes from VAE or from inspired VA, and for blood-flow inhomogeneity as well, although the effect is smaller there (Fig. 3, 4A, 4B; figures not read).
- It is curtailed at extremes of V/Q with significant dead space.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
None (not an opioid paper). No MEAC, ke0, tPeak or potency ratio.

Mechanism: this is the concentrating effect (Stoelting and Eger). Rapid uptake of one gas concentrates the other gas in the alveolus. The effect is strongest in low-V/Q compartments, and inhomogeneity magnifies it. Only one gas of the pair can be augmented at a time. Augmentation occurs when the gas's own uptake is low relative to its inspired-to-mixed-venous gradient.

## Caveats
- Only two compartments. The limiting case of absorption atelectasis imposes a 50% shunt (p.8).
- Steady state only. Distribution of both ventilation and blood flow at once was not modeled (p.9).
- Equal-solubility hypothetical gases. The authors say gases of different solubility may behave in a more complex way (p.9).
- Dantzker et al. is cited for absorption atelectasis at VAI/Q below 0.1.
