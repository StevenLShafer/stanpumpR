# Levison 1977: in-vitro activity and clinical efficacy of clindamycin in anaerobic infections

## Study
Single-centre report (Medical College of Pennsylvania) in a 1977 J Infect Dis supplement, summarising earlier work
(refs 6 and 9):
- **In vitro**: 29 clinical Bacteroides fragilis isolates (VPI identification), broth dilution (method in ref 6).
  Agents: clindamycin, rosamicin, josamycin, metronidazole, erythromycin.
- **Clinical**: 32 patients with anaerobic pleuropulmonary (16) or intra-abdominal/pelvic (16) infections given
  clindamycin 300-600 mg every 8 h (route not stated for this group), plus surgery and, for aerobic Gram-negatives,
  usually gentamicin. Separate cohort of 37 inpatients on parenteral clindamycin watched for diarrhoea/colitis;
  31 of them received 1.8 or 2.4 g/day.

## Model and parameters
No pharmacokinetics measured. In-vitro results (PDF p1 abstract, printed S49; PDF p2 Results, printed S50):
- Inhibition of 29 B. fragilis: at 0.8 ug/mL, 100% josamycin and metronidazole, 93% rosamicin, 97% clindamycin,
  76% erythromycin; at 1.6 ug/mL, 100% clindamycin and rosamicin; erythromycin 3.1 ug/mL inhibited 97%.
- Killing (abstract only): metronidazole bactericidal against 97% of isolates (concentration not stated);
  clindamycin or rosamicin at 1.6 ug/mL bactericidal against 80%. Results text: metronidazole most bactericidal,
  clindamycin = rosamicin, erythromycin and josamycin least. No MIC/MBC differences between B. fragilis subspecies.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
- In-vitro end points: inhibition (MIC) and bactericidal activity (MBC, definition in ref 6).
- Clinical: cure in 27 of 32 (14/16 pleuropulmonary, 13/16 intra-abdominal/pelvic; S52). Mean fever duration after
  starting clindamycin 3.5 days (infiltrative pneumonitis), 6.5 days (other pleuropulmonary), 9.6 days (range 6-20;
  intra-abdominal/pelvic, after excluding drained/drug-fever patients).
- Adverse: diarrhoea (>= 5 loose stools/day) in 11 of 37 (30%), onset mean 3.6 days; more common with abdominal or
  pelvic infection (10/15 vs 2/22, P < 0.01); one patient with a colonic plaque (pseudomembranous colitis). Mild CPK
  rises in 10 of 28; drug fever in 2; one icteric hepatitis.
- No concentration measured in patients; no PK/PD target. Not an opioid paper.

## Covariates
None.

## Caveats
- The in-vitro data are a summary of Santoro 1976 (ref 6); no table, MBC definition or distribution here.
- Uncontrolled case series; most patients also had surgery/drainage and many got other antibiotics.
- 1970s isolates: clindamycin resistance in B. fragilis is now far more common (the repo notes 25-60%).

## Verification against stanpumpR
- **1.6 mg/L killed 80% of 29 isolates**: matches the abstract (PDF p1, printed S49), confirmed on the rendered page;
  ug/mL = mg/L, no conversion issue. **matches**.
- The row puts an MBC80 in the MBC90 column; the Basis and Note say so explicitly, and this value is the band's
  upper edge (max core MBC90) for clindamycin (`R/antibioticMbc.R:412-414`). The paper does not give the MBC90, so the
  true MBC90 for these isolates is above 1.6 mg/L; the repo's choice is disclosed, not a transcription error.
- The Note's "the highest killing level reported at a concentration serum reaches" is the repo's own framing; this
  paper reports no serum concentrations. "MBC is 4 to 128 times the MIC" and the other Note claims come from the other
  cited sources (Zabransky 1973, Klepser 1996, Boiten 2024), not this paper.
- The clinical dosing (300-600 mg every 8 h; 1.8-2.4 g/day parenteral) is consistent with the label range the row
  quotes, but the repo does not use this paper for dosing. No PK reported, so nothing to compare with the clindamycin
  PK model.
