# Sanders 2017: Connected consciousness after tracheal intubation (isolated forearm technique)

## Study
Prospective, pragmatic, international cohort at six centres (Wisconsin, Waikato, Aachen, Michigan, Groningen, Liege), Aug 2014-Aug 2015 (PDF pp.2-3). 260 adults (18-88 y, 51 +/- 16) having general anaesthesia with tracheal intubation; anaesthetic technique left to the clinician. A forearm cuff was inflated to 50 mmHg above systolic pressure during induction, before the muscle relaxant, so the hand stayed unparalysed (train-of-four with four twitches confirmed). An independent observer asked the patient to squeeze the hand just before laryngoscopy and again after securing the tube (about 1 min after intubation), and if the patient responded, to squeeze twice for pain. Postoperative modified Brice and Bauer questionnaires within 24 h. NCT02248623.

None: this is an incidence study, no pharmacokinetic model.

Key numbers (all counts or means from the paper, not derived unless marked):
- Post-intubation IFT responders 12 of 260 = 4.6% (PDF p.1, p.6); 5 of the 12 reported pain by a double squeeze (42% of responders; 1.9% of the cohort; derived 5/260).
- Pre-laryngoscopy responders 5 (1.9%); combined pre- plus post-laryngoscopy 6.2% (PDF p.7 area, Results).
- 18 patients moved spontaneously after laryngoscopy and were counted as non-responders. No explicit recall in 253 questioned.
- Responders were younger: 39 +/- 17 vs 51 +/- 16 y (P = 0.01). Signs of sympathetic activation 50% vs 2.4% (P = 0.03).
- Depth-of-anaesthesia monitor (BIS or Neurosense; n = 154): pre-laryngoscopy 54 +/- 20 vs 42 +/- 14 (P = 0.02); post-intubation 57 +/- 25 vs 43 +/- 16 (P = 0.02) (Table 3, PDF p.7, rendered). Individual ROC areas 0.69 and 0.66, not significant.
- Volatile anaesthetic before intubation (not TIVA, n = 229): 72.4% of non-responders vs 45.5% of responders (Table 4); age-adjusted odds ratio of responding 0.2 (0.1-0.8), P = 0.02.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
End point: behavioural response to command (hand squeeze) in the isolated forearm, taken as connected consciousness. No ke0, EC50, MEAC or effect-site concentration is reported. Drugs were given at the clinician's discretion; doses in Table 4 (PDF p.7) are mean +/- SD, nonresponders vs responders:
- Propofol induction dose 2.0 +/- 0.7 vs 2.3 +/- 0.5 mg/kg (P = 0.11). (The Results text cites an age-adjusted odds ratio of 1.4 per mg/kg for propofol, P = 0.47.)
- Fentanyl-equivalent dose 1.4 +/- 1.1 vs 1.5 +/- 1.1 ug/kg (P = 0.79), excluding remifentanil infusions (n = 234). The conversion factors to fentanyl equivalents are not given in the text read.
- Midazolam given in 50.4% vs 33% (P = 0.38); ketamine 10.1% vs 25% (P = 0.13); all three responders given ketamine (25 mg) reported pain.
- Responders with and without pain did not differ in fentanyl equivalents (P = 0.81).

Opioid audit: no MEAC, no ke0, no tPeak, no potency ratio is stated in this paper; the fentanyl-equivalent dose is a bolus total on an unstated conversion.

## Covariates
Logistic regression of IFT response on age plus one drug variable at a time (age-adjusted). Age and absence of pre-intubation volatile agent raised the odds of response; propofol and ketamine dose did not (adjusted).

## Caveats
Underpowered for multivariable analysis (12 events, against about 100 expected); the authors call the secondary results hypothesis-generating. The IFT misses patients who are aware but do not respond, and the intubation time point was chosen to avoid spontaneous movement. Questioning for recall within 24 h is early. Uncontrolled, pragmatic doses and timing (induction to intubation 4.5-5.4 min on average). The abstract and Table 3 disagree on one depth-of-anaesthesia number (see open question).
