# Kazama 1999: the BIS and blood-pressure keOs of propofol, and what EEG activation does to them

Not a source of any stanpumpR value — nothing in the repository cites it — and read for a
different reason: it is the cleanest quantitative demonstration of why **propofol carries no
EEG-index pharmacodynamic endpoint** (Steven L. Shafer, 2026-11-26; whether any drug should is
queued for a future session, and no drug has one today. The reasoning is in the header of
`R/pdEffect.R` and on `inst/app-help/models/percent-effect.md`).

The three propofol cards that kept citing "BIS-derived ke0 values" as evidence against the
library's tPeak of 1.6 min (`PMID_15220769.md`, `PMID_16129974.md`, `PMID_23157660.md`) were
citing quantities of this kind. This paper shows, in the same patients on the same monitor, that
such a quantity is not a property of the drug.

## Study

41 patients aged 20-85, in four age groups — 20-39, 40-59, 60-69 and 70-85 — with 10 or 11 per
group contributing to the keO analysis (Table 1 gives 11, 12, 12 and 12 patients enrolled).

A target-controlled infusion held **four sequentially increasing, randomly selected plasma
propofol concentrations from 1 to 12 mcg/mL**, each for about 30 min (PDF p. 1). Group 4 could
not be taken above 7 mcg/mL because of excessive anaesthesia levels (Fig. 2 caption, p. 5). Six
patients were excluded for overshoot — more than ±30% of the individual equilibrated
concentration at 0.5 or 1.0 min, or ±10% between 2 and 30 min.

The TCI ran on **whole-blood** propofol parameters; the median performance error between plasma
and target was 22.3% and the median absolute performance error 22.9%, which the authors ascribe
to the roughly 30% difference between whole-blood and plasma concentration. Measured equilibrated
plasma concentration correlated with target at r = 0.973.

**How keO was obtained.** Not from a hysteresis collapse but from the approach to steady state:
with the plasma held at a pseudo-steady-state target, a monoexponential was fitted to the effect
against time after each step. The first 30 s of each record were discarded, because **BIS is a
30-s rolling window, which the authors note delays the value the monitor reports** (PDF p. 8)
— their own observation, and the reason a ke0 fitted to BIS is partly a measurement of the
monitor.

## Model and parameters

No pharmacokinetic model is fitted and none is reported; the PK is the TCI's. What the paper
estimates is a first-order equilibration half-time per effect measure, per age group, split by
whether the EEG was in its activation phase.

**The finding that matters here.** The equilibration half-time for BIS is **2.3 min when the EEG
is not in its activation phase and about 3.8 min when it is** — one drug, one monitor, one index,
in one cohort. The two sets are different concentration steps, not the same step measured twice:
the activation figures come from the second step alone and the others from the second, third or
fourth, so concentration is not held across the comparison. That is the point and not a flaw in
it, because activation happens at particular concentrations, so an investigator's choice of step
decides which number comes out. The authors put the ratio at **1.6 to 1.8 times, P < 0.05** (PDF
p. 7), and attribute it plainly (p. 9): the EEG activation seen at the start of the
second step is what slowed the fall in BIS.

So a published "BIS ke0" for propofol is not one number. It depends on which part of the biphasic
EEG response the measurement caught, which depends in turn on the concentration step the
investigator chose. The paper says so of the phenomenon generally in its introduction: the EEG
shows activation followed by slowing, and "one EEG variable value may be associated with two
different anesthetic concentrations."

## Pharmacodynamics and end points

Three measures, deliberately compared: **BIS**, **SEF95** and **systolic blood pressure**.

- **BIS against SBP.** The effect on BIS is much faster. Without EEG activation the SBP
  half-times are about 2.5 times the BIS ones below 60 years and about four times above 60
  (PDF p. 7). SBP's half-times rise with age (5.68, 5.92, 8.87, 10.22 min) while BIS's do not
  (2.31, 2.30, 2.29, 2.37); the BIS half-times did not differ significantly between age groups.
- **EEG activation does not affect SBP.** The SBP half-times with and without activation were the
  same, which is what one expects if activation is an EEG phenomenon rather than a change in the
  drug's delivery to its site.
- **SEF95 is the weakest of the three.** Its keO could not be defined at the third or fourth step
  because the decreases were too small, and Figure 6 shows no relation between SEF95 and propofol
  blood concentration.
- **Below 2 mcg/mL no keO could be computed from BIS at all**, the index not being linear there
  (PDF p. 8).
- **IC50s (Table 2, rendered) separate the brain from the circulation.** The BIS IC50 does
  NOT fall with age -- 5.60, 6.76, 8.21, 7.67 mcg/mL, which the paper reports as not
  significantly different -- while the SBP IC50 falls steeply, 4.61, 4.13, 3.96 and **2.09
  +/- 0.19** mcg/mL, the last significant against all three younger groups. On this measure
  the elderly brain is no more sensitive to propofol; the elderly circulation is, by more
  than twofold. These are PLASMA concentrations, not effect-site.

## Covariates

Age, as four groups rather than a continuous covariate. No weight, sex or other term is fitted.
The paper's own conclusion is that age does not change the rate of BIS reduction but does change
the blood-pressure response.

## Caveats

- **Nothing here is a stanpumpR parameter**, and this paper is not a candidate to become one. It
  is evidence about what an EEG-derived effect-site rate constant means.
- **The abstract states the elderly blood-pressure finding backwards.** It reports the SBP
  IC50 as significantly greater in the elderly; Table 2 gives group 4's as 2.09 +/- 0.19
  against group 1's 4.61 +/- 0.51. Settled against the rendered table, not merely suspected;
  see *open_questions*.
- **BIS is a 30-s rolling window.** The authors discard the first 30 s to deal with it, which
  works for their design but is not available to anyone fitting a ke0 to a BIS trace in general.
- **The TCI used whole-blood parameters**, so the plasma concentrations against which everything
  is expressed carry a median performance error of 22.3%.
- **Tables 1 and 2 are scrambled in the text layer**, which is why both were rendered here.
  Never quote either from the extracted text: it puts the sex ratio on Table 1's 'number of
  patients' row and the confidence limits on Table 2's IC50 row.
- Group 4 was capped at 7 mcg/mL, so the elderly contribute no data at the top of the range.

## Verification against stanpumpR

- **No library value comes from this paper**, so there is nothing to reconcile. **Not applicable.**
- **It does not contradict tPeak 1.600.** Schnider 1999's 1.6 min is the visually assessed time of
  peak EEG effect on the canonical univariate parameter; Kazama's 2.3 min is a BIS equilibration
  half-time from a monoexponential fitted during a pseudo-steady-state step, on an index with a
  30-s rolling window, and it becomes 3.8 min if the step happens to catch the activation phase.
  These are different quantities and the larger one is not a better estimate of the smaller.
- **It supports the decision of 2026-11-26** that propofol carries no EEG-index endpoint, and is
  the quantitative case for it: one drug, one monitor, one index, and an apparent equilibration
  HALF-TIME that is 2.3 min or 3.8 min according to whether the EEG was activating -- a ke0 of
  0.30 or 0.18 /min respectively (derived, ln2 over the half-time). The concentration is
  NOT held across that comparison -- the steps are different steps, and activation itself occurs
  at particular concentrations -- which is the point rather than a confound: a single BIS ke0
  does not describe the drug, because the number depends on where in the concentration range,
  and so on which limb of the biphasic response, the investigator measured it.
