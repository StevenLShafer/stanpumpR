# Mann 2022: FDA mechanistic model of opioid overdose, naloxone dosing and cardiac arrest

## Study
Quantitative systems pharmacology model, not a clinical trial. Four linked parts (Fig 1, p 1021): (1) compartmental PK of opioid and
naloxone giving free concentration in the effect compartment; (2) competitive binding of opioid and naloxone at the mu receptor
(kon, koff, n); (3) PD in which opioid-bound receptor, not naloxone-bound, reduces all three ventilatory drives (central
chemoreflex, peripheral chemoreflex, wakefulness); (4) a physiology model (Ursino lineage) of O2/CO2 exchange, ventilatory control
and blood-flow control, extended so acute severe hypoxaemia gives first a rise in cardiac output, then decompensation and cardiac
arrest. Cardiac arrest = total blood flow 0.01 L/min (p 1022).

In vitro: [3H]-labelled fentanyl and nine derivatives (carfentanil, sufentanil, butyryl-, 4-fluorobutyryl-, 4-fluoroisobutyryl-,
furanyl-, isobutyryl-fentanyl, alfentanil, remifentanil), plus buprenorphine and naloxone, in association/dissociation assays; naloxone
competition assays for validation (Fig 5; no alfentanil or remifentanil competition data). Assay details are in the Supplement.

Calibration data: Algera fentanyl PK model (refs 28, 29); two human fentanyl ventilation studies (ref 22 bolus 0.5 mg, room air;
ref 11 opioid-naive and chronic users, end-tidal CO2 about 50 mmHg, doses of 75/150 and 250/350 ug per 70 kg). Validation:
in vitro competition (median predicted/observed 1.00, IQR 0.86-1.00), animal hypoxia-arrest data (equine; median 1.00, IQR 0.92-1.06),
human ventilation with fentanyl and alfentanil infusions (Mildh, ref 23) and remifentanil (Olofsen, ref 24) (median 0.95, IQR 0.90-1.01;
p 1026, Fig 6). Two-fold agreement was the stated criterion. A virtual population of 2,000 patients was drawn from PK models (Table S1)
and binding-parameter distributions (Table S2).

Simulations (p 1022, Fig 7): IV bolus fentanyl 1.63 mg (medium) and 2.97 mg (high), estimated from about 500 fatal fentanyl overdoses
(ref 26); carfentanil 0.012 and 0.022 mg, scaled from the ratio of the minimum cardiac-arrest doses (derived: 1.63/0.012 = 136 and
2.97/0.022 = 135). Simulated chronic opioid users. Naloxone 2 mg IM given 1 min after ventilation fell to 40% of baseline, as the
2 mg/2 mL generic product or the 2 mg/0.4 mL autoinjector; 1.5 h simulated.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
Opioid audit:
- **MEAC / minimum effective or analgesic concentration:** none reported. The model's end points are minute ventilation, PaO2, PaCO2,
  cardiac output and cardiac arrest, not analgesia; the authors say analgesia would need other mechanisms (p 1030).
- **ke0 / t1/2ke0 / tPeak / hysteresis:** none in the main text. PK gives "free concentrations in the effective compartment" (Fig 1
  legend), but the effect-compartment rate constants are in the Supplement. The text says only that ventilatory depression after carfentanil
  develops more slowly than after fentanyl, attributed to carfentanil's slower association (p 1028); no end-point time constant.
- **Receptor kinetics (end point: in vitro binding, not a clinical effect):** dissociation t1/2 buprenorphine 84.4 min > carfentanil 46.6
  > sufentanil 10.7 > remifentanil 5.6 > all other fentanyls tested <3 min (p 1022, Table S2). Carfentanil kon is also slower than fentanyl's.
- **Equianalgesic / potency ratio:** none for analgesia. A cardiac-arrest "dose equivalence" is used: carfentanil/fentanyl dose ratio about
  1/136 (derived above) for the minimum dose causing cardiac arrest in the model (the ratio itself is in the Supplement).
- Simulated outcome: 2 mg/0.4 mL IM naloxone gave 16% (IQR 14-17%) cardiac arrest after medium fentanyl and 20% (IQR 19-22%) after medium
  carfentanil; 2 mg/2 mL gave 30% (IQR 28-31%) after medium fentanyl (p 1029, Fig 7b). A single dose of either product did not prevent
  arrest in the typical patient after high-dose carfentanil (p 1028). Chronic opioid users needed a higher fentanyl dose than naive subjects for
  similar depression (Fig 4e,f, p 1024).
- PD parameters (receptor occupancy to drive reduction) were estimated on fentanyl and assumed identical for the other fentanyls (p 1030).

## Covariates
None stated in the main text (virtual-population variability in PK and binding parameters; opioid-naive vs chronic user as a PD distinction).

## Caveats
Authors: "validation" means fitness for the prediction task; carfentanil and other illicit fentanyls have no human PK, so only fentanyl and
carfentanil were simulated; PD parameters assumed common to all fentanyls; simple competitive binding ignores G-protein activation and
receptor states (buprenorphine's partial agonism is not captured); brain-hypoxia injury without arrest is not an end point.
Mine: carfentanil PK is an adaptation of fentanyl PK to a half-life, not a measurement; overdose doses are derived, not observed; every
key parameter table is in the Supplement, which this PDF lacks.
