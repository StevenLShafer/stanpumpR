## Study
A concentration-response analysis of the **Cross-National Collaborative Panic Study** (CNCPS), a multicentre,
double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of alprazolam in panic disorder whose clinical results were published
separately (the paper's ref 7). Conducted at **five sites in the United States, two in Canada and one in
Australia** (PDF p2, printed 716). The CNCPS was sponsored by The Upjohn Co; the analyses in this paper were
supported by NIH grant MH-34223 (PDF p8).

Patients met DSM-III criteria for agoraphobia with panic attacks or panic disorder, assessed by structured
interview, aged 18-64 and otherwise in good physical health; those with depressive symptoms entered only if those
were secondary to, or chronologically preceded by, the panic disorder.

**237 patients** were evaluated at week 3 and **219** at week 8. Three were deleted post hoc for unverifiable
characteristics: two with extreme body weights (27.7 and 154.5 kg) and one with a plasma alprazolam level of
360 ng/mL at week 8. Mean age 37 y, mean weight 69.1 kg, and **two-thirds of them were women** (Results, PDF p2).

**Dosing.** A 1-week drug-free period, then randomisation to alprazolam or identical placebo. 1.0 mg capsules in
evenly divided doses **up to four times daily**, titrated upward every 3-5 days on a standardised but flexible
schedule until maximum observable benefit or dose-limiting side effects; titration complete by day 19, with every
effort to reach **6.0 mg/d**. After the week-3 evaluation physicians could titrate up or down to optimise
response, to a maximum of 10 mg/d. Daily dose was estimated over the 7 days preceding each evaluation.

**Sampling.** Venous samples at screening, baseline, week 3 and week 8. **No attempt was made to standardise the
time of sampling relative to the last dose** - the authors list this as a limitation and argue it is mitigated by
the up-to-four-times-daily schedule, which flattens interdose fluctuation. Total alprazolam in plasma by gas
chromatography, assayed blind in a single laboratory (Tufts/New England Medical Center); diazepam and
desmethyldiazepam quantitated at every time point.

Normalising daily dose for body weight **worsened** the correlation (r .46 → .35 at week 3, .59 → .50 at week 8).
Read precisely, that compares two single-predictor regressions and says dose per kilogram is the weaker of the
two — which is evidence against CL/F being proportional to total weight, since strict proportionality would make
dose per kilogram the *better* predictor. It does **not** show that weight explains none of the residual
variability: that would need weight entered alongside dose, or a comparison of residuals, and the paper reports
neither.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
End points at weeks 3 and 8: patient- and physician-rated global improvement on a 1-10 integer scale (5 = no
change, 10 = back to normal), change from baseline on the Hamilton Anxiety and 17-item Hamilton Depression
scales, complete remission of panic attacks (none in the week before evaluation), and a structured side-effect
interview scored 0-3. No ke0, tPeak, EC50, hysteresis or potency ratio.

**The paper gives two sizes for that side-effect list**, both confirmed on the rendered page: the Methods say "a
list of **37** possible side effects was provided to patients" and the next paragraph says only five of the
**42** effects were significantly more frequent on alprazolam than placebo (PDF p3, printed 717). The paper never
reconciles them and does not describe two item sets, so this card reports both rather than picking one. Nothing
turns on it: the five that differed are named, and they are the sedative ones.

**Week 3 — the concentration-response week** (dose had not yet been adjusted to response). All figure values
below confirmed on the rendered pages:

- **Global improvement and both Hamilton scales peak at 20-39 ng/mL and are no greater above** (Figure 2, PDF p5):
  patient global F = 5.9, P<.001; physician global F = 4.6, P<.005; Hamilton Anxiety F = 4.61, P<.005; Hamilton
  Depression F = 3.2, P<.025. Student-Newman-Keuls: for patient global the 20-39 category differed from the other
  three, which did not differ from one another; for physician global and both Hamilton scales the <20, 40-59 and
  >=60 categories did not differ from each other, and 20-39 differed from <20.
- **Spontaneous panic attacks**, complete remission (n = 133; Figure 3, PDF p5): **31.3% (n=16) below 20 ng/mL,
  71.7% (46) at 20-39, 75.0% (36) at 40-59, 62.9% (35) at >=60**; chi-square 10.6, P<.02. Note the shape — it
  peaks at 40-59 and **falls** at >=60, which neither the abstract nor the Comment mentions.
- **Situational panic attacks** (n = 159 stated, 158 across the four bars; Figure 3): 20.0% (n=20), 36.0% (50),
  33.3% (48), 45.0% (40); chi-square 3.8, **not significant**.
- **Either or both types** (n = 192; Figure 4, PDF p6): 16.0% (n=25), 32.8% (64), 32.1% (56), 48.9% (47);
  chi-square 8.4, P<.04.
- **Sedative side effects rise with concentration.** Only 5 of 42 elicited effects were more frequent on
  alprazolam than placebo, all sedative: sedation, ataxia, slurred speech, fatigue, weakness. 9.2% of patients
  reported severe CNS-sedative effects and 40.6% moderate or severe. **14.5% severe at >=60 ng/mL against 7.2%
  below** (P<.1). The mean composite sedation score rose in the two upper categories, F = 4.14, P<.01 (Figure 5,
  PDF p6); the moderate-or-severe frequency is not monotonic across the four bins (about 37, 31, 45, 51%) but is
  clearly higher above 40 ng/mL.

**Week 8 — nothing.** No significant relation of concentration to global improvement, either Hamilton scale,
remission frequency, or side effects. The authors attribute this to clinicians having titrated dose to response
between weeks 3 and 8, which raises response, lowers side effects and **obscures the concentration-response
relationship**. Remission at week 8 was 64% (situational), 81% (spontaneous), 60% (either/both); severe
CNS-depressant effects fell to 3.2%, moderate or severe to 25.9%.

The authors' conclusion: at least **20 ng/mL** is needed for global improvement and suppression of spontaneous
panic attacks in short-term treatment, weighed against side effects that rise with concentration; levels >=60
ng/mL *may* be needed for situational attacks, which they state is not statistically supported. They define
"subtherapeutic" as **<20 ng/mL** (PDF p8) and propose plasma monitoring to separate undertreatment from
drug-caused side effects.

## Covariates
None modelled. Body weight was tested on the dose-concentration relation and made it worse. Prior benzodiazepine
use was measured rather than modelled: diazepam plus desmethyldiazepam above an arbitrary 10 ng/mL in 21% of
patients at screening and baseline, falling to 6.7% (placebo 15.5%) at week 3 and 4.1% (placebo 9.2%) at week 8;
deleting those patients did not meaningfully alter any concentration-response relationship.

## Caveats
- **Sampling time was not standardised** relative to the last dose, so concentrations mix points across the
  dosing interval. Up-to-4x daily dosing limits, but does not remove, the damage.
- **Compliance was not verified**, and concentrations of 0 ng/mL appear at both weeks, so some patients were
  certainly not taking the drug.
- The concentration-response relation exists only at week 3. By week 8 dose had been titrated to response — **an
  exposure-response analysis in a response-titrated trial measures the titration, not the drug**, as the authors
  recognise.
- Four coarse bins, the highest open-ended (>=60 ng/mL), so the paper cannot locate an upper bound beyond "no
  further benefit above about 40".
- Situational-attack remission rises but not significantly, and the suggestion that >=60 ng/mL helps is
  explicitly unsupported statistically.
- Short-term (8 weeks) treatment of panic disorder in outpatients aged 18-64. Nothing here speaks to chronic
  anxiolysis, the elderly, or the procedural sedation most of stanpumpR's benzodiazepine models concern.
- **Three internal inconsistencies**, all confirmed on rendered pages, none affecting anything stanpumpR uses:
  the abstract's slopes (11.7, 10.8) differ from the Results and Figure 1 (11.8, 10.6); the text's "75% reached
  4.0 or 5.0 mg/d" at week 3 does not match Table 2, where 4 and 5 mg/d together are 63.3% (70.0% counting every
  dose of 4 mg/d or more); and Figure 3's situational-attack bars sum to 158 patients where the text says 159.
- No pharmacokinetic parameters: this constrains no volume or half-life, though the slope implies an apparent
  oral clearance (below).

## Verification against stanpumpR
**not-applicable** at master bbae1aa9: no repository location cites this paper, which is the verdict the Olkkola
card (`PMID_7605420.md`) uses in the same situation. The independent checks below all pass; they are recorded
here rather than as a `matches` verdict because there is no cited location to match against.

- **The dose-concentration slope reproduces the library's clearance for its REFERENCE MAN — and that is the
  point at issue, not a clean validation.** `R/drugs_alprazolam.R` gives CL/F 3.50 L/h at its 70 kg, 170 cm, 35 y
  reference **man**, which is **11.9 ng/mL per mg/day** at steady state (verified by running the model at
  master). This paper measured **11.8** at week 3 and 10.6 at week 8. Equivalently CL/F = 1 mg/day / 11.8 ng/mL =
  **3.53 L/h** (derived), 0.051 L/h/kg at the 69.1 kg cohort mean.

  The agreement with the male reference is under 1%, but **that is not the model's prediction for this cohort**.
  The default model scales to fat-free mass, so its prediction depends on sex and height as well as weight, and
  two thirds of these patients were women, for whom it gives 14.46 ng/mL per mg/day at 70 kg, 170 cm and 37 y —
  and 13.7 to 14.2 for the cohort as a mix, under the assumed heights and swept weight gap set out in the
  `[decision]`, which is a sensitivity scenario rather than a cohort prediction because the paper reports no
  heights. Matching total weight (69.1 against 70 kg) therefore does not make this a like-for-like comparison. So
  the honest reading is a fork, not a validation: either the model's clearance is right and its FFM-driven sex
  difference is too large, or the measured slope is biased low (uncontrolled sampling time, unverified
  compliance, concentrations of 0 ng/mL). The card cannot choose. **Steven L. Shafer resolved the fork on
  2026-10-30 by leaving the scaling as it is and documenting the choice** — see *The sex scaling, decided* below.

  What survives either way is the order of magnitude: three sources agree on alprazolam's apparent oral clearance
  to within about 10% — DeVane 1993 (the model, 3.50 L/h at the male reference), Ciraulo 1990 (3.9,
  `PMID_2407754.md`) and this paper (3.53 at week 3, 3.93 at week 8). The sampling caveat that weakens Ciraulo's
  version is weaker here: samples scattered through the dosing interval estimate the average steady-state
  concentration better than a near-trough sample does, not worse.

- **It is consistent with retaining the band's upper limit, and gives no reason to raise it.** AGNP's ceiling is
  44 ng/mL. Global improvement and both Hamilton scales are maximal at 20-39 ng/mL with no further benefit above,
  in 237 patients; spontaneous-attack remission peaks at 40-59 and falls at >=60; sedative side effects keep
  rising. **The bins cannot locate 44 ng/mL**: 44 sits inside the 40-59 category, so nothing here distinguishes
  it from 41 or from 59, and the caveat above says as much. What the paper does establish is that benefit stops
  increasing somewhere around 40 and harm does not — which is a reason to keep a ceiling in that region, not
  evidence for 44 in particular. The library's band needs no change. The band's lower half (5-19 ng/mL) is this paper's
  "subtherapeutic" range, but that is not a conflict: AGNP's lower limit describes the concentrations expected on
  ordinary doses, not an efficacy threshold, and the CSV's own notes say so.

- **It closes the `[decision]` on `papers/digests/PMID_2407754.md`.** Ciraulo 1990's responder percentages rise
  across and above the band (33/67/73/81% of *samples*), which that card raised as a possible reason to revisit
  the ceiling. This study is nine times the size, uses continuous end points rather than a responder dichotomy,
  and finds a plateau and then a fall. The two reconcile: Ciraulo binned repeated weekly evaluations during an
  open escalation, so its gradient partly tracks time on treatment, and its top bin is open-ended. **The band
  stands.** The Ciraulo card is updated accordingly in the same pull request.

Nothing in the plotted curve depends on this paper.

## The sex scaling, decided
**Steven L. Shafer decided on 2026-10-30: stay with the current scaling, and explain the choice in the
documentation.** `pkSizeFactors()` continues to normalise every patient's fat-free mass to the **reference man's**
54.5 kg, so a woman of the same weight, height and age is scaled down — at 70 kg, 170 cm and 35 y her factors are
0.824 on the volumes and 0.865 on the clearances (recomputed at master e4145b5f). Nothing in the code, the
parameters or the tests changed. The note explaining why is a new subsection of `docs/weight-adjustment.md`,
*Why the reference is a man*; the short form is that the reference is an **anchor, not a claim** about which
patient is typical, that moving to a same-sex reference would not remove a sex difference but silently assert a
different one (that women clear drug in proportion to their smaller fat-free mass *and* a further correction
back to parity), and that it would change every scaled model in the library on the evidence of one uncited
exposure-response study in which the heights needed to compute fat-free mass were never reported.

What this card measured stands unchanged and is the reason the note exists: the model's default (FFM on) predicts
**14.46** ng/mL per mg/day for a woman at 70 kg, 170 cm, 37 y against this paper's measured **11.8** in a cohort
two-thirds women, where the reference man's **11.90** matches it almost exactly. That remains a real tension and
the note says so rather than resolving it — the alternative readings (a slope biased low by uncontrolled sampling
and unverified compliance; or an FFM-driven sex difference larger than the alprazolam data support) are both
still open, and settling them needs a study that reports heights.

The discarded alternative is recorded so it is not reopened from scratch: normalising each patient's fat-free
mass to a **same-sex** reference would keep the obesity correction that Abernethy 1984 requires for this drug
(oral clearance was *not* higher in obese subjects, so a total-weight model is wrong) while removing the
across-sex rescaling. It was not adopted. Note also that the three available positions disagree in **direction**,
not merely in size, which is why no choice here is neutral: DeVane's own fitted sex term would put a woman at
7.5 ng/mL per mg/day, no sex effect at all puts her at 11.9, and FFM scaling puts her at 14.5.
