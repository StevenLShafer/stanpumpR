# Greenblatt 1993: plasma alprazolam against efficacy and side effects in panic disorder

Supplied by Steven L. Shafer on 2026-10-29 and filed by hand, after `papers/digests/PMID_2407754.md` (Ciraulo 1990)
flagged it as the stronger alprazolam exposure-response paper and recorded that it was missing from the archive.

## Study
A concentration-response analysis of the **Cross-National Collaborative Panic Study** (CNCPS), a multicentre,
double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of alprazolam in panic disorder whose clinical results were published
separately (the paper's ref 7). Patients met DSM-III criteria for agoraphobia with panic attacks or panic
disorder, assessed by structured interview, aged 18-64 and otherwise in good physical health; patients with
depressive symptoms entered only if those were secondary to, or preceded by, the panic disorder.

**237 patients** were evaluated at week 3 and **219** at week 8. Three were deleted post hoc for unverifiable
characteristics: two with extreme body weights (27.7 and 154.5 kg) and one with a plasma alprazolam level of
360 ng/mL at week 8. Mean age 37 y, mean weight 69.1 kg, two thirds women (Results, PDF p2, printed 716).

**Dosing.** A 1-week drug-free period, then randomisation to alprazolam or identical placebo. 1.0 mg capsules in
evenly divided doses **up to four times daily**, titrated upward every 3-5 days on a standardised but flexible
schedule until maximum observable benefit or dose-limiting side effects; titration complete by day 19, with every
effort to reach **6.0 mg/d**. After the week-3 evaluation physicians could titrate up or down to optimise
response, to a maximum of 10 mg/d.

**Sampling.** Venous samples at screening, baseline, week 3 and week 8. **No attempt was made to standardise the
time of sampling relative to the last dose** - the authors list this as a limitation and argue it is mitigated by
the up-to-four-times-daily schedule, which flattens interdose fluctuation. Assayed blind at Tufts/New England
Medical Center; diazepam and desmethyldiazepam quantitated at every time point.

## Model and parameters
No pharmacokinetic model and no pharmacokinetic parameters: this is linear regression of steady-state
concentration on daily dose, plus ANOVA and chi-square of outcome against stratified concentration categories.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
This is the paper's substance. End points at weeks 3 and 8: patient- and physician-rated global improvement on a
1-10 integer scale (5 = no change, 10 = back to normal), change from baseline on the Hamilton Anxiety and the
17-item Hamilton Depression scales, complete remission of panic attacks (no attack in the week before the
evaluation), and a structured side-effect interview over 37 possible effects scored 0-3.

**Week 3 — the concentration-response week** (dosage had not yet been adjusted to response):

- **Global improvement and both Hamilton scales were maximal in the 20-39 ng/mL category, and no greater above
  it** (ANOVA significant across categories; Figure 2, PDF p5). Student-Newman-Keuls: for patient global ratings
  the 20-39 category differed from the other three, which did not differ from one another; for physician global
  ratings the <20, 40-59 and >=60 categories did not differ from each other and 20-39 differed from <20.
- **Spontaneous panic attacks** (133 patients with complete data; 87, or 65%, in remission at week 3): remission
  above 20 ng/mL was more than twice that below, with only small differences among the three categories above
  20 ng/mL - **72% at 20-40 ng/mL against 31% below 20** (Comment, PDF p7). Significant by chi-square.
- **Situational panic attacks** (159 patients; 57, or 36%, in remission): remission rose from 20% below 20 ng/mL
  to 45% at >=60 ng/mL, but **not significantly**. The authors suggest, without statistical support, that higher
  levels may be needed for situational attacks.
- **Either or both** (192 patients; 66, or 34%): remission rose significantly across categories (Figure 4).
- **Side effects rose with concentration.** Only 5 of 42 elicited effects were more frequent on alprazolam than
  placebo, all sedative: sedation, ataxia, slurred speech, fatigue and weakness. At week 3, 9.2% of patients
  reported severe CNS-sedative effects and 40.6% moderate or severe. **14.5% had severe sedative effects at
  >=60 ng/mL against 7.2% below** (P<.1), and the mean composite sedation score differed significantly across
  categories, rising in the two highest (Figure 5).

**Week 8 — nothing.** No significant relation of concentration to patient or physician global improvement, to
either Hamilton scale, to remission frequency, or to side effects. The authors attribute this to clinicians having
titrated dose to response between weeks 3 and 8, which raises response, lowers side effects and **obscures the
concentration-response relationship**. Remission at week 8 was 64% (situational), 81% (spontaneous) and 60%
(either/both); severe CNS-depressant effects fell to 3.2%, moderate or severe to 25.9%.

The authors' conclusion: at least **20 ng/mL** is needed for global improvement and for suppression of spontaneous
panic attacks in short-term treatment, weighed against side effects that rise with concentration; plasma
monitoring may have a clinically useful role during short-term treatment.

No ke0, no tPeak, no hysteresis, no effect-compartment model, and no potency ratio.

## Covariates
None modelled. Body weight was tested on the dose-concentration relation and did not improve it. Prior
benzodiazepine use was measured rather than modelled: diazepam plus desmethyldiazepam above an arbitrary 10 ng/mL
was found in 21% of patients at screening and baseline, falling to 6.7% (against placebo's 15.5%) at week 3 and
4.1% (against 9.2%) at week 8; deleting those patients did not meaningfully alter any concentration-response
relationship.

## Caveats
- **Sampling time was not standardised** relative to the last dose, so the concentrations are a mixture of points
  across the dosing interval. The authors argue the up-to-4x daily schedule limits the damage; it does not remove
  it.
- **Compliance was not verified.** The authors list this first among the study's disadvantages.
- The concentration-response relation exists only at week 3. By week 8 dose had been titrated to response, which
  the authors correctly identify as self-obscuring - **an exposure-response analysis in a response-titrated trial
  measures the titration, not the drug**.
- The efficacy categories are four coarse bins, and the highest is open-ended (>=60 ng/mL), so the paper cannot
  locate an upper bound beyond saying there is no further benefit above about 40 ng/mL.
- Situational panic attacks show a rising but non-significant trend; the suggestion that >=60 ng/mL may help them
  is explicitly not statistically supported.
- Short-term treatment (8 weeks) in panic disorder. Nothing here speaks to chronic anxiolysis, to the elderly, or
  to the procedural sedation most of stanpumpR's benzodiazepine models concern.
- **Two internal inconsistencies**, both confirmed on rendered pages, neither affecting anything stanpumpR uses:
  the abstract's slopes (11.7, 10.8) differ from the Results and Figure 1 (11.8, 10.6); and the text's "75%
  reached 4.0 or 5.0 mg/d" at week 3 does not match Table 2, where 4 and 5 mg/d together are 63.3% (70.0% if
  every dose of 4 mg/d or more is counted). The likeliest reading is that 75% reached 4 or 5 mg/d at some point
  during titration while Table 2 gives the dose at the week-3 evaluation, but the paper does not say so.
- No pharmacokinetic parameters: this constrains no clearance, volume or half-life directly, though the slope
  implies an apparent oral clearance (below).

## Verification against stanpumpR
**not-applicable** at master bbae1aa9: no repository location cites this paper. Three things it settles or bears
on:

- **It supports the band's upper limit.** AGNP's ceiling is 44 ng/mL. This paper finds global improvement and both
  Hamilton scales maximal at 20-39 ng/mL with **no further benefit above**, in 237 patients, while sedative side
  effects continue to rise (14.5% severe at >=60 ng/mL against 7.2% below). A ceiling in the low 40s is exactly
  what that supports. The library's band needs no change on this evidence.

- **It closes the `[decision]` on `papers/digests/PMID_2407754.md`.** Ciraulo 1990's responder percentages rise
  across and above the band (33/67/73/81% of *samples*), which that card raised as a possible reason to revisit
  the ceiling. This study is nine times the size, measures continuous end points rather than a responder
  dichotomy, and finds a plateau. The two are reconcilable: Ciraulo binned repeated weekly evaluations during an
  open escalation, so its gradient partly tracks time on treatment, and its top bin is open-ended. **The band
  should stand.** The Ciraulo card is updated accordingly in the same pull request.

- **The dose-concentration slope corroborates the library's clearance, from a third independent source.** At
  11.8 ng/mL per mg/day, CL/F = 1 mg/day / 11.8 ng/mL = 84.7 L/day = **3.53 L/h** (derived; week 8's 10.6 gives
  3.93 L/h). `R/drugs_alprazolam.R` is an apparent-scale oral model from DeVane 1993 and gives **3.59 L/h** at the
  70 kg reference patient - within **2%** of the week-3 figure, and this study's mean weight was 69.1 kg, so the
  comparison is at very nearly the right size. The same caveat as on the Ciraulo card applies and is weaker here:
  the samples are scattered through the dosing interval rather than taken near a trough, which makes the mean a
  better estimate of the average steady-state concentration, not a worse one. Three sources - DeVane 1993 (the
  model), Ciraulo 1990 (3.9 L/h) and this paper (3.5-3.9 L/h) - now agree on alprazolam's apparent oral clearance
  to within about 10%.

Nothing in the plotted curve depends on this paper.
