# Girard 1998: Markov mixed-effect model for drug compliance

PDF page = printed page minus 2312 (PDF p. 1 = printed 2313).

## Study
- Statistical methods paper with one worked example. No pharmacokinetics and no drug concentrations.
- Data (Section 4.1, PDF p. 8): 21 HIV-positive patients from ACTG 175, assigned to zidovudine (ZDV) three times daily, monitored 4 to 15 weeks with MEMS2+ electronic pill-bottle caps (openings taken as dosing events). Dose-time vectors were 70 to 320 events per patient, 3521 events in total.
- Data are split into nominal dose times (4429 in all; text layer, PDF p. 8): 1066 with no dose, 3211 with one dose, 146 with two, 6 with three. The 6 triple-dose events were folded into n=2 for p(n) and dropped for p(delta|n).
- Covariates used: age (years); time-varying weekend, midday and evening indicators (3 a.m. begins the "day").
- Estimation: first stage estimates each patient's habitual nominal dose times (Section 2); second stage is maximum likelihood by FOCE/Laplace in NONMEM. Model check is a posterior predictive check on longest drug holiday (LDH) and non-therapeutic time (NTT, gap beyond an assumed 8 h duration of action), M=200 replications, PDF pp. 14-16.

## Model and parameters
Hierarchical Markov model: p(n) x p(delta|n). n is the number of doses (0, 1 or 2) taken at a nominal dose time. Conditional on covariates and the individual's random effects, it depends on the previous n and on no other dosing event (the Markov hypothesis; Abstract, Section 3.1). delta is the timing error, multivariate normal conditional on n, the covariates and the random effects. The dose-count model itself carries covariates (Table II), so unconditional dose-count probabilities cannot be read from the previous n alone.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
None. No concentration, effect, ke0, EC50 or MEAC. The paper is about patient adherence, not drug action. It assumes a duration of action of 8 h (the dosing interval) only to define NTT, and notes that using 12 h moves the minimum and median NTT toward the centre of their reference distributions (PDF p. 16; text layer).

## Covariates
Age (centred at 42 y, scaled by 20; a >45 y indicator on timing variance), time of day (morning is the reference), weekend vs weekday, and the previous number of doses taken. Text (PDF pp. 11 and 13): doses are taken on average 51, 21 and 25 minutes later at morning, midday and evening on weekends than on weekdays; weekends raise within-subject timing SD by about 10 per cent and age over 45 lowers it by about 20 per cent (Table III, theta4 110%, theta5 77%).

## Caveats
- Example is 21 patients, one drug; the authors call it an illustration.
- Nominal dose times are estimated from the data in a first stage and then treated as known; the authors say this is the main criticism. One patient showed only two modes, so the "midday" dose was never intended.
- The normal model for timing errors is a poor fit for some patients; observed NTT and LDH fall in the left tail of the predictive distributions (PDF p. 15-16).
- Table I (percentages and totals by previous dose) was not confirmed on an image, so its numbers are not recorded here.
