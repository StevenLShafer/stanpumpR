# Morishima 1992: cocaine in pregnant and nonpregnant rats, and why its cholinesterase result points away from a human adjustment

An intravenous cocaine infusion in chronically catheterised pregnant and nonpregnant rats, measuring
cardiac output and regional blood flow, cocaine in plasma and tissues, placental transfer, and plasma
cholinesterase activity. In stanpumpR it is not a pharmacokinetic source: it is the only direct
evidence behind the cocaine row of the pregnancy registry, and what it supports there is a decision
to make **no** adjustment.

**Why this card was written by hand.** `digest_run.py` records this paper as refused by the model's
safeguards even when batched alone. The refusal is at card-writing only — the PDF, its extracted
text and its context file were filed normally. See *Caveats*.

## Study
Sprague-Dawley rats, 10-12 weeks old: **29 pregnant of known gestation and 31 nonpregnant**, in
three groups (PDF p. 2, printed 70):

- 17 term pregnant and 18 nonpregnant for the blood-flow study;
- 6 term pregnant and 6 nonpregnant for plasma cholinesterase activity;
- 6 term pregnant and 7 nonpregnant as a validation group, checking that the microsphere injections
  and blood sampling of the first two groups did not themselves distort the result.

Catheters were placed under xylazine-ketamine into the left ventricle via the right carotid, the
abdominal aorta via the left femoral artery, and the superior vena cava via the femoral vein, then
tunnelled between the scapulae; the animals recovered 2-3 days. On the study day each rat sat in a
semidark closed cardboard box rather than a restraint holder, acclimatising 2-3 h. Cardiac output
and organ blood flow were measured with about 100,000 15-micrometre microspheres labelled with
46Sc, 57Co or 103Ru, injected into the left ventricle, with a reference sample from the aorta.

**The dose is a subtoxic infusion, not a bolus:** cocaine **0.33 mg/kg/min intravenously**. After
7-10 min every animal became restless with sniffing and repetitive grooming, and more agitated
towards the end, but **no animal had seizures or respiratory or cardiovascular depression** (PDF
p. 4, printed 72) — which is what makes the comparison one of subtoxic exposure.

Cocaine was measured to a limit of 1 ng/mL from 1 mL of plasma; fetal samples were run at 0.1 mL, so
their limit was 10 ng/mL (PDF p. 3). Analysis was two-way ANOVA with repeated measures or t tests,
Tukey for multiple comparisons, significance at p < 0.05.

**Read Table 1's footnote before reading its columns.** The superscript marks a difference from that
group's own BASELINE, not a difference between pregnant and nonpregnant animals. So the paper's
central claim — that pregnancy enhances the cardiovascular response — rests on the pregnant group
falling significantly from its own baseline in heart rate, cardiac output, brain, heart, uterine and
placental flow, where the nonpregnant group's changes are not marked. It is not a direct test of
pregnant against nonpregnant at the end of the infusion.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
The end points are haemodynamic and behavioural, not an effect-site model: heart rate, mean arterial
pressure, cardiac output, regional blood flow by microsphere, and an unscored behavioural
description. There is no EC50, no ke0 and no effect compartment, and the paper fits no
concentration-effect relation of any kind. Cocaine lowered heart rate and cardiac output and raised
mean arterial pressure, with brain, myocardial, uterine and placental flow all falling significantly
in the pregnant animals.

## Covariates
None fitted. Pregnancy is the grouping variable, not a covariate in a model, and the only other
measured difference is body weight (the pregnant rats being heavier by the weight of the conceptus).
Nothing here can be carried into a covariate model.

## Caveats
- **Rats, and the species caution is the authors' own.** They report that across 12 species plasma
  cholinesterase activity ranges from over 6,000 to as low as 200 mU/mL, primates highest and sheep,
  goat and cattle lowest, and that although sheep have 5-6 times LOWER activity than the rat,
  cocaine is metabolised significantly FASTER in sheep (PDF p. 9, printed 77). Their conclusion is
  that hydrolysis via cholinesterase may not be the only mechanism. So this paper's own authors say
  that cholinesterase activity does not predict cocaine hydrolysis across species — which is the
  single most important thing in it for stanpumpR, and it is in the Discussion, not the abstract.
- **The cholinesterase change runs the opposite way to the human one.** Activity was HIGHER in the
  pregnant rat (2,193.7 against 1,441.4 mU/mL, confirmed at 2,240.6 against 1,427.3 in the
  validation group), where human pregnancy LOWERS butyrylcholinesterase. The rat's lower maternal
  plasma cocaine is consistent with its own raised enzyme; neither direction transfers.
- **No pharmacokinetic parameter exists here.** No clearance, volume or half-life, so nothing can be
  scaled, and the fetal:maternal ratios are concentration ratios at one infusion, not transfer
  constants.
- **Small groups.** Six pregnant and six nonpregnant animals in the cholinesterase comparison.
- **A single subtoxic infusion rate**, so nothing about dose dependence, and the authors note the
  animals were agitated by the end — a state that itself alters cardiac output.
- **Route matters, and the authors say so.** They warn against intraperitoneal dosing in pregnant
  rodents, having seen unexpectedly high cocaine in intestine, myometrium, placenta, amniotic fluid
  and fetuses shortly after an intraperitoneal injection, because organs are exposed directly from
  the peritoneal space. A comparison with intraperitoneal mouse work [their ref. 4] is set aside on
  that ground.
- **A mechanism pointing the other way is raised and not resolved:** pregnancy lowers protein
  binding for several drugs, and if that held for cocaine the greater free fraction might raise
  rather than lower the risk. The paper calls the protein binding of cocaine controversial.

## Verification against stanpumpR
The registry row makes four checkable claims about this paper. All four hold.

- `Morishima 1992 infused IV cocaine in pregnant vs non-pregnant rats` -- cocaine 0.33 mg/kg/min
  intravenously to chronically catheterised pregnant and nonpregnant rats. **Matches.**
- `found LOWER maternal plasma cocaine in pregnancy` -- the abstract states it, and the validation
  group gives the paired figures, 1.62 against 2.09 mcg/mL. **Matches.**
- `with enhanced cardiovascular response` -- Table 1: heart rate, cardiac output and brain, heart,
  uterine and placental flow all fall significantly from baseline in the pregnant animals, and the
  nonpregnant group's corresponding changes are not marked significant. **Matches**, with the
  footnote nuance above: the comparison is each group against its own baseline.
- `limited placental transfer (fetal/maternal 0.28)` -- Table 2 gives 0.278 +/- 0.048 for plasma,
  with brain 0.120 and heart 0.190; all fetuses were alive at delivery. **Matches**, to three
  decimals.
- The row's mechanism note, that falling butyrylcholinesterase `could in principle slow` hydrolysis
  but is `opposed by the rat finding`, is **correct and understated**. The rat's cholinesterase is
  not merely unchanged but significantly RAISED in pregnancy, about 52% above nonpregnant, and the
  authors offer that as their explanation for the lower maternal plasma cocaine. The row's
  conclusion of `no adjustment` is therefore not just cautious: the paper's authors explicitly argue
  that cholinesterase activity does not predict cocaine hydrolysis across species, having found
  sheep metabolising cocaine faster than rats on 5-6 times less enzyme. **Consistent**, and the full
  text strengthens the row rather than changing it.
- The paper supplies **no pharmacokinetic parameter**, and the repo takes none from it. **Consistent.**
- Nothing in the paper contradicts a value stanpumpR holds.
