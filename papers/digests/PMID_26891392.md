# Ansari 2016: PK/PD of drugs commonly used in pregnancy and parturition (review)

Page convention: the PDF is the journal article, printed pp. 786-804 = PDF pp. 1-19, so PDF page = printed page − 785.

## Study
Narrative review (Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology section, Stanford; CME article), no systematic
search or meta-analysis. Part 1 (printed pp. 786-790 / PDF pp. 1-5) covers the physiology of pregnancy that alters
bioavailability, distribution, clearance and half-life, and placental transfer. Part 2 (printed pp. 790-799 /
PDF pp. 5-14) goes class by class with eight summary tables, each giving per drug: dose adjustment, fetal/maternal
(F/M) ratio, breast-milk/maternal plasma ratio, former FDA category, PK/PD alteration and the reference numbers.
F/M is used for UV/MA or UA/MA alike (printed p. 790 / PDF p. 5), and many ratios are collated from two textbooks
(ref 14, Campbell and San Vicente in Shnider and Levinson, 2013; ref 37, Briggs and Freeman, 2014). The table rows
list their references as a set; the review does not say which reference supports which cell, so the "refs" below
are the row's reference list unless the text cites a number at the specific statement. 209 references.
Disclosure: Shafer was Editor-in-Chief; Flood is married to Shafer; handled by a guest editor (printed p. 799).

## Model and parameters
No pharmacokinetic model and no parameter estimates (no clearances in L/min, no volumes in L). The review gives
directions of change and a handful of magnitudes (ratios, half-lives, percentages). Every quantitative claim, with
its source:

### Physiology (Part 1)
- Gastric emptying unchanged before labour; delayed in labour by pain, anxiety, opioids (printed p. 787 / PDF p. 2, refs 6, 7).
- CYP2D6 induced in pregnancy; ultrarapid codeine metabolisers make high morphine peaks (printed pp. 787-788 / PDF pp. 2-3, ref 8).
- Plasma volume about +50% by term; water-soluble drugs give lower peak and steady-state concentrations at unchanged dosing (printed p. 788 / PDF p. 3, ref 31).
- Albumin falls from the second trimester; plasma protein, and binding capacity, 70-80% of prepregnancy at delivery (printed p. 788 / PDF p. 3, ref 32); free fraction rises for midazolam, digoxin, phenytoin, valproic acid.
- Renal blood flow and GFR +50% by the second trimester, staying up until 3 months postpartum (printed p. 788 / PDF p. 3; no reference number on the sentence). This is the review's own statement and the only GFR figure it gives.
- Hepatic share of cardiac output unchanged; CYP2D6, CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9 and UGT induced; CYP1A2 activity reduced, caffeine concentration doubled in the third trimester (printed p. 789 / PDF p. 4, ref 47).
- Half-life: both V and CL rise, so the change is unpredictable and each drug must be studied (printed p. 789 / PDF p. 4).
- Placenta expresses CYP1A1, 2E1, 3A4, 3A5, 3A7, 4B1, 19; dexamethasone and prednisone undergo significant placental metabolism (ref 74); remifentanil is hydrolysed by placental esterases, fetal concentrations an order of magnitude below maternal (ref 72) (printed p. 790 / PDF p. 5).
- Cardiac output +35% by end of first trimester, +50% by end of second, may double again at delivery; SVR −20% at term; arterial pressure −20% by 20 weeks (printed p. 797 / PDF p. 12).
- Plasma cholinesterase −30% from week 10 to 6 weeks postpartum (printed p. 793 / PDF p. 8).
- PT and PTT about 20% shorter in normal pregnancy (printed p. 797 / PDF p. 12).

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
Opioid audit: no MEAC or minimum analgesic concentration, no ke0, t½ke0, tPeak or hysteresis, and no equianalgesic or
potency ratio anywhere in the review. The only opioid concentration is fentanyl's peak maternal concentration of
0.5 ng/mL with epidural dosing (Table 3, printed p. 791 / PDF p. 6, refs 14, 56-58; not a pregnancy comparison).
Buprenorphine's adjusted odds ratio of 2.55 compares neonatal withdrawal with methadone (Table 3).
Other PD: propofol C50 for loss of consciousness unchanged in early pregnancy (Table 4, ref 108); other sedative
hypnotics have more PD activity in pregnancy, possibly from progesterone (printed p. 792 / PDF p. 7, no figure);
thiopental more efficacious despite lower concentrations (Table 4); possibly higher neuraxial local-anaesthetic
sensitivity (printed p. 794, refs 136-139); metoprolol more effective at lower concentrations (Table 1); magnesium
target ≥4 mEq/L (printed p. 797). Ropivacaine toxic threshold 2.2 µg/mL (printed p. 794, ref 126).

## Covariates
None in the modelling sense. The covariate the review describes is pregnancy itself (mainly late pregnancy and labour
for the opioid and antibiotic data, first trimester for propofol C50, ondansetron and doxylamine), multiple gestation
for corticosteroids, and BMI for the cefazolin dose (ref 150). No covariate equations.

## Caveats
- A narrative review: most figures come from small single studies or textbooks (refs 14, 37), and the table cells
  do not say which reference gave which value.
- Some cells are imprecise or inconsistent: morphine's PK/PD cell has no verb ("clearance >70%"), and its dose cell asks
  for an *increased* dosing interval alongside a shorter half-life; the text (printed p. 792) says methadone and
  buprenorphine are metabolised by CYP3A4 and CYP2B6 "respectively" while Table 3 gives CYP3A4 and CYP2B6 for methadone
  and CYP3A4 for buprenorphine; remifentanil fetal concentrations an order of magnitude below maternal (text p. 790)
  sit beside a table F/M of 0.29-0.88.
- Oxycodone's 3.8 h comparator is not identified as nonpregnant in the table; the table cell reports labouring women.
- Magnitudes are lower bounds or ranges ("more than doubles", ">70%", "8-20 h"); no variability is given.
- No volume figure is given for any drug even where an increase is stated (antibiotics, enoxaparin, thiopental).
- Shafer is an author and the library's author: the review is the library's own source for pregnancy.
