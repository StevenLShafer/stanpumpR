# Ciraulo 1990: alprazolam dose, steady-state plasma concentration and response in panic disorder

## Study
Parallel, double-blind, placebo-controlled outpatient trial at two sites - University of Connecticut Health Center
(site 1) and Lutheran General Hospital, Chicago (site 2). **37 patients enrolled, 36 analysed**, randomised 2:1 to
alprazolam (n = 25) or placebo (n = 11) and kept in that arm for the whole 14-week period. All had agoraphobia
with panic attacks or panic disorder with limited phobic avoidance (DSM-III), with at least one panic attack a
week for the three weeks before entry. Excluded: current or prior psychoactive substance dependence, major
affective illness, psychosis within 6 months, acute suicidal ideation, bipolar disorder, dementia, epilepsy or
seizures, and clinically important laboratory or organ disease. Psychotherapy and behavioural therapy were
withheld during the study.

Overall the patients were 20-57 years old and 72% women (26 of 36, which the table reproduces).

**Dosing** followed a fixed protocol schedule of 1 mg tablets (Table 1, PDF p2): 1 mg/day in week 1, rising weekly
to **10 mg/day in week 8** (2-3-2-3 across morning, noon, evening and bedtime), then tapering by roughly 1 mg/week
to 0 by week 13. Escalation was interrupted only for intolerable side effects, in which case the dose returned to
the previous level for 7 days and a second attempt was made; failing that, the last tolerated dose was recorded as
that patient's maximum. **Doses were not adjusted for clinical response, and were increased even when the
investigator judged maximum effect reached** - which is what makes the dose-concentration relation clean.
Dosing records were checked against returned tablet counts; two sequences were recorded as missing.

**Sampling and assay.** Two 10 mL samples at each weekly visit, **8-10 h after the last dose**. One analysed by
HPLC for 4-hydroxyalprazolam and alpha-hydroxyalprazolam; the other by gas-liquid chromatography with electron
capture detection for intact alprazolam, and for diazepam and desmethyldiazepam to confirm prior therapy had
stopped (one patient still had subtherapeutic, falling levels and was retained).

## Model and parameters
No pharmacokinetic model: no clearance, volume or half-life is estimated. The analysis is linear regression of
steady-state concentration on daily dose, run separately for escalation, taper and the two combined, and
separately by site ("each study site gave similar slopes").

For comparison the authors quote an earlier **inpatient** study (their ref 4, Smith and Kroboth): mean steady state
29.6 ng/mL at 3 mg/day, 61.5 at 6 mg/day and 102.9 at 9 mg/day - the same roughly 10 ng/mL per mg. Those are not
this study's measurements.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
The end point is anxiety, rated weekly: Clinician Rated Anxiety Scale (CRAS), Patient Rated Anxiety Scale (PRAS)
and a Physician Global Rating. **Response is defined as a 50% improvement in CRAS score from baseline.** There is
no ke0, no effect-compartment model and no hysteresis analysis; concentration is taken at the time of the
evaluation.

- Significant correlations with alprazolam concentration during **ascending** dose sequences: negative for CRAS and
  PRAS, positive for Physician Global.
- During **descending** sequences, a significant but small negative correlation between total side-effect severity
  score and concentration (R² = 0.078, slope = 0.063, p = 0.0026).
- Systolic pressure against concentration R² = 0.027, slope 0.061, p = 0.0054; diastolic R² = 0.015, slope 0.033,
  p = 0.0381. No consistent change in pulse or laboratory values.
- The responder gradient (Figure 3) is the headline: **33% → 67% → 73% → 81%** across 0-20, 21-40, 41-60 and
  60+ ng/mL. The authors state significant response in patients who achieved 20 ng/mL and maximum response in the
  60 ng/mL and above group.

**The authors disclaim it themselves**: the study was not designed to determine clinical outcome against plasma
level, the effects of time were not controlled, and the data "should thus be considered preliminary". Because the
dose rose weekly, a higher concentration also means longer on treatment.

**Tolerability.** Doses up to 6 mg/day were well tolerated by 80% of the alprazolam subjects; 40% (10 of 25)
reached 10 mg/day, against 27% (3 of 11) of placebo patients reaching 10 tablets/day. The principal cause of
intolerance was sedation. Because escalation stopped when side effects appeared, no relation between plasma level
and side effects could be established. Mean maximum side-effect severity during ascent was 19.1 ± 10.6 on
alprazolam at a mean 3.0 ± 3.6 mg/day, against 23.7 ± 13.9 on placebo.

**Withdrawal.** No severe withdrawal symptoms at a taper of about 1 mg/week after a mean 47.3 ± 15.6 days of
treatment; headache was the only new symptom; 12 of 25 had rebound anxiety (CRAS above baseline), most often at
the step from 1 mg to 0.

## Covariates
None modelled. Site was examined and gave similar slopes. Age, weight and sex are tabulated but not related to
concentration.

## Caveats
- **Not designed for concentration-response**, and the authors say so; concentration and time on drug rise
  together under a fixed escalation, so the responder gradient cannot separate them.
- Small: 25 patients on drug, and the 60+ ng/mL band is the tail of an escalating regimen.
- Concentration is a single 8-10 h post-dose sample on a 3-4 times daily regimen - a trough-ish value, not an
  average steady state, and the AGNP sampling convention for alprazolam is 10 h after the last dose, which is
  close.
- Response is a 50% CRAS improvement, a responder dichotomy, not a continuous effect measure.
- Outpatients with panic disorder, so the concentrations are those of chronic oral anxiolysis, not of the
  procedural sedation stanpumpR's benzodiazepine models are mostly about.
- No pharmacokinetic parameters at all: nothing here constrains a model's clearance or volume.
