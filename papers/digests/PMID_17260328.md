# Pacheco 2007: pharmacokinetics of corticosteroids during pregnancy (narrative review)

## Study
A four-page narrative review with 21 references, written from an NICHD Obstetric-Fetal Pharmacology Research Units
network. It covers chronic glucocorticoid use in pregnancy and deliberately leaves out antenatal steroids for fetal
lung maturity (PDF p 1). It reports no new data, no systematic search and no pooled analysis.

## Model and parameters
No model. These are the numbers the review restates, all secondary:

Pregnancy physiology (PDF pp 1-2):
- Cardiac output rises 40-50%, 75% of the rise by 12 weeks.
- Blood volume rises 40-50%, from 6 weeks to a plateau at about 30 weeks.
- Albumin concentration falls 25%, though total albumin is stable.
- GFR rises 50% by the end of the first trimester.
- Liver blood flow is unchanged.
- CBG is raised; total and free cortisol rise.

Glucocorticoid PK in nonpregnant adults (PDF pp 2-3):
- Half-lives (ref 8, Goodman and Gilman): prednisolone 2.2 ± 0.5 h, prednisone 3.6 ± 0.4 h, methylprednisolone
  2.3 ± 0.5 h.
- Renal elimination is 10-30%.
- Prednisolone binding is 95% below 100 µg/L and 60% above 700 µg/L. Hypoalbuminaemia lowers binding from about 65%
  to about 45%.
- Estrogen or oral contraceptives increase prednisolone half-life 2.5-fold.
- Methylprednisolone binding is linear, 77%.
- **Hydrocortisone** binding is non-linear like prednisolone's, so its volume of distribution rises at higher doses
  (ref 15) (PDF p 3).
- **Dexamethasone** half-life is prolonged in liver disease and shorter in renal disease, with a higher metabolic
  clearance rate (ref 10). That is the only dexamethasone PK statement in the review, and it is not about pregnancy.

Glucocorticoids in pregnancy (PDF p 3), all about **betamethasone**:
- Petersen 1983 (ref 18): clearance and volume are both higher than in nonpregnant subjects, so the half-life is
  unchanged. The higher clearance may come from fetoplacental metabolism, since there is no evidence of increased
  renal or hepatic clearance.
- Ballabh 2002 (ref 19): in twins, half-life is 20% shorter and clearance 32% higher than in singletons. There was no
  difference between 24 and 34 weeks.
- Hakkola (ref 21): CYP3A4 is expressed in first-trimester placenta.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
None quantified. Nuclear glucocorticoid receptors are about 40% saturated at physiological cortisol (ref 6). Biological
activity lasts 12-36 h, longer than the plasma half-life. The authors speculate that raised baseline cortisol in
pregnancy might limit the response to exogenous glucocorticoid.

## Covariates
None quantified.

## Caveats
- The "Theoretical assumptions" section (PDF pp 3-4) is the authors' speculation. One example: they expect a
  dose-dependent rise in volume and clearance with little change in half-life. These are not findings.
- The review gives no dexamethasone pregnancy data and no hydrocortisone pregnancy data.

## Verification against stanpumpR
- `R/drugs_dexamethasone.R:47-50`: **cannot verify the Ansari attribution here**. Ansari's Table 8 is not part of this
  card. Against Pacheco itself: the review gives **no dexamethasone figure in pregnancy**. Three things in the header
  do have a counterpart in Pacheco, and all three are about **betamethasone**:
  - a higher clearance with the half-life unchanged (Petersen);
  - fetoplacental or placental metabolism as the possible cause;
  - 32% higher clearance in twins (Ballabh), which matches "a multiple gestation needing more still".

  Pacheco does not support a doubling of clearance for any drug. Whatever Ansari's Table 8 takes from Pacheco for
  dexamethasone is an extrapolation from betamethasone. The library's numbers rest on Tsuei 1980 and Krzyzanski 2023,
  so no parameter is affected.

  betamethasone/dexamethasone pattern" overstates it. The clearance-and-volume-up, half-life-unchanged pattern is given
  only for betamethasone (ref 18). The review's one dexamethasone sentence is about liver and renal disease.
