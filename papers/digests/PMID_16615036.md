# Nedelman 2006: diagnostics for confounding in PK/PD (oxcarbazepine)

## Study
Statistical methods paper. It asks whether a conventional (observed-concentration) PK/efficacy relationship from a dose-controlled trial can stand in for the true concentration-effect relationship, to support pediatric bridging. Two oxcarbazepine adjunctive-therapy trials are used as the example.
- Drug and analyte: oxcarbazepine, with concentrations of the active metabolite MHD (10-monohydroxy derivative). Concentration measure is Cmin, the average steady-state trough of MHD (about 12 h post-dose), in micromol/L (Fig 2 axis).
- Study A (adults, ref 3): randomized 600, 1200 or 2400 mg/day (2400 reduced to 1800 by amendment) or placebo, given b.i.d. Troughs were observed at up to 6 visits.
- Study P (children, ref 4): weight-based target daily dose (Table I): 900 mg for 20.0-29.0 kg, 1200 mg for 29.1-39.0 kg, 1800 mg for >39.1 kg. Cmin was inferred from a population 1-compartment first-order-absorption model, evaluated at 12 h post-dose at steady state.
- Response: Y = log(100(S-B)/B + 110), where S and B are seizure frequency per 28 days in the maintenance and baseline phases.
- n per study and the assay are not given in the text I read.

## Model and parameters
Efficacy models (section 4.2; coefficients are estimate +/- SE):
- Study A, eq 10, Y = b0 + b1*C + b2*C*[log(B) - 2.5]: b0 = 4.58 +/- 0.04, b1 = -0.0098 +/- 0.0011 per micromol/L, b2 = 0.0027 +/- 0.0009.
- Study P, eq 11, Y = b0 + b1*C: b0 = 4.53 +/- 0.06, b1 = -0.0077 +/- 0.0015. The fuller model gave b1 = -0.0070 +/- 0.0030, b2 = 0.00029 +/- 0.00094, and baseline frequency was not significant (p > 0.3).
- The authors say b1 differs by about 20% between adults and children.
- Dose/PK model (eq 15, both studies): log(C) = g0 + g1*log(Dose/BSA) + g2*AED, where AED indicates carbamazepine, phenobarbital or phenytoin co-medication (enzyme inducers that raise MHD clearance). Coefficients are not tabulated.
- Diagnostics: Rosenbaum-Rubin sensitivity to a simulated confounder (Tables IV, V), residual correlation (Fig 3: r = 0.069, p = 0.25, Study A; Fig 4: r = 0.0044, p = 0.96, Study P), and two-stage least squares with a Hausman test (Tables VI, VII).
- Table VI, Study A: OLS b1 = -0.010 +/- 0.001, TSLS b1 = -0.011 +/- 0.001, Hausman p = 0.47. Table VII, Study P: OLS b1 = -0.0078 +/- 0.0015, TSLS b1 = -0.0084 +/- 0.0018, Hausman p = 0.78.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
End point is the log-transformed percent change in seizure frequency from baseline. No ke0, EC50, Emax or MEAC is reported, and no therapeutic range is stated. Fig 2 plots percent change against Cmin from 0 to about 120 micromol/L. Not an opioid paper.

## Covariates
Baseline seizure frequency modifies the Study A slope (interaction p < 0.001). Weight enters the Study P dose assignment. BSA and enzyme-inducing AEDs enter the Dose/PK model (eq 15). Tables II and III list correlations of age, height, weight, BSA, creatinine clearance, gender, interacting AEDs and AED counts with Cobs and Yobs. The largest was 0.15 in Study A; some reached about 0.3 in Study P. Study P rows of Table III were scrambled in the text layer.

## Caveats
- The authors say none of the evidence is conclusive, and confounding cannot be excluded without randomized concentrations.
- Conventional PK/efficacy slopes were estimated imprecisely.
- The 2006 PDF is a pre-publication copy ("in press"), so the final journal pagination is unknown.
