# Peter 2000: methylene blue kinetics after IV and oral dosing

## Study
- **Design:** randomised three-way crossover, at least 1 week apart (p247). Arms: 100 mg (313 µmol) methylene blue
  (MB) IV over 30 s; 100 mg orally (two 50 mg gelatin capsules); and 100 mg orally with 800 mg mesna.
- **Population:** 7 healthy volunteers (4 men, 3 women), 19-53 y. Weight is not reported.
- **Sampling:** venous whole blood (EDTA) at 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 120 and 240 min. Urine in fractions
  for 24 h (p248). Glass tubes, because MB adsorbs to plastics.
- **Assay:** HPLC of whole-blood haemolysate at 660 nm, linear 9-900 nmol/L, day-to-day CV 13.6% at 10 nmol/L.
  Urine by spectrophotometry before and after oxidation of the leucoform (p248).
- **Rats:** 10 mg/kg IV or intraduodenal (4 rats each), with tissues taken at 1 h (p248).
- **Analysis:** trapezoidal AUC, CL = dose/AUC, mean ± SEM. Multiple regression on the pooled IV blood
  concentrations (p248).

## Model and parameters

in µmol/L, R² = 0.99:

Derived from the printed equation (my arithmetic):
- C(0) = 13.5 µmol/L, an apparent V1 of 313/13.5 = 23 L.
- AUC0-∞ = 9.4/0.237 + 3.8/0.065 + 0.3/0.0022 = 234.5 µmol·min/L. 58% of it lies in the terminal term, beyond
  the 240 min of sampling.
- AUC0-240 = 154 µmol·min/L, close to the observed 134 (a pooled fit against a mean of individual AUCs).
- Dose/AUC0-∞ = 1.33 L/min (80 L/h). Dose/(mean AUC) = 313/134 = 2.3 L/min, against the printed mean of
  individual ratios, 3.0 L/min.
- 313 µmol per 100 mg implies 319.5 g/mol: the dose is expressed as the chloride salt.

The fitted curve checks against Fig. 1 (about 5.9 µmol/L at 5 min and 0.18 at 240 min, against the plotted
means).

**Abstract versus Results:** the abstract gives the IV AUC as 137 nmol/min/ml and the oral AUC as 9. The Results
(rendered p3) give 0.134 ± 0.025 and 0.010-0.011 µmol/min/ml. The units, printed "/min/", are a slip for
µmol·min/mL.

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
- No human effect end point.
- Rats (Fig. 3, p249): after intraduodenal dosing, MB was not detectable in blood at 1 h, and brain concentrations
  were lower than after IV dosing. Intestinal wall and liver concentrations were higher (P < 0.05). Bile
  concentrations were similar.
- The authors conclude that "first-pass distribution", not poor absorption or first-pass metabolism, explains the
  low oral blood levels (pp249-250).

## Covariates
None.

## Caveats
- 7 subjects, mean ± SEM, and sampling to only 4 h. The terminal rate (0.0022/min) is set by the last two
  sampling times and by urinary excretion from 4 h onwards (urinary half-life 6.6 h).
- Whole blood by HPLC at 660 nm. Walter-Sack 2009 (LC-MS/MS) argues that earlier assays measured only unbound MB.
  That would make these concentrations, and the near-zero oral bioavailability they imply, too low. The paper
  itself proposes tissue sequestration.
- The reported clearance (3.0 L/min) uses the truncated AUC, so it overstates the clearance that the paper's own
  triexponential would give (1.33 L/min; derived).
