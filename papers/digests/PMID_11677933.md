# Yano 2001: mixed-effects vs naive models for dichotomous data

## Study
Simulation study (NONMEM), no patients and no drug. Dichotomous 0/1 responses are generated from a logistic model, logit = theta1 + theta2*x + eta_i, with eta_i ~ N(0, omega^2) (Eq. 1-2; printed p.392). Variant with a random slope (Eq. 1*; omega2^2 = 0.25, i.e. 50% CV on slope). Continuous variants: r = p + eps (Eq. 3, sigma^2 = 0.25) and a Michaelis-Menten/Emax form (Eq. 4-7) used to repeat a 1980 simulation (N=50, n=3).
- Parameter sets: theta1 = 0 or 0.85 (typical response probability 0.5 or 0.7); omega^2 = 0.5 or 2 (Table I, p.392). With a covariate theta1 = 0, theta2 = 1.
- Designs: balanced N = 40 or 120 with n = 2 or 10 per subject (Table II); unbalanced 75%/25% mixes (Table III); dose-escalation designs with n = 5 (escalate while running mean response < 0.6).
- 150 replications per experiment. Estimators: naive pooled data (NPD, ordinary logistic regression), Laplacian ML (LAP), Monte Carlo "Almost Sure" ML, and an approximate REML. Measures: mean error, RMSE, RMASE.

## Findings
- ML mixed-effects (LAP) RMSE for fixed effects is often no better, sometimes worse, than naive pooling (Fig. 2, 5, 6, 8; pp.399-403).
- ME-favourable conditions the authors list: N of about 100 or more, n not much below 10 for at least 25% of subjects, omega^2 about 1 or more, and typical p of about 0.7 or higher (p.407).
- Dose-escalation designs favour ME even at N = 40 if n >= about 5 and omega^2 >= about 1 (p.407, Fig. 10).
- REML tracks NPD more closely but needs more computation (Fig. 13-14, pp.410-411).
- The more linear the model, and the more variability sits on linear parameters, the more ME helps (pp.408-409).
