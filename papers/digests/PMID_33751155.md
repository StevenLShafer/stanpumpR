# Heinonen 2021: sertraline in pregnancy and placental transfer (MAGDALENA RCT substudy)

## Study
- **Design:** pharmacokinetic part of a double-blind randomised trial (MAGDALENA, NCT02185547) of sertraline vs
  placebo, both with internet-based CBT, in pregnant women with untreated moderate major depression, Stockholm,
  May 2016 to March 2019 (p2).
- **Population:** 16 women enrolled (10 sertraline, 6 placebo); 9 who ended on sertraline with two or more
  concentrations were analysed, and 7 mother-infant pairs for transfer (p4). Women with comorbidity or chronic
  drug treatment were excluded. Maternal age 26-39 in the sertraline group (Table 1, p5). No maternal weight,
  CYP2C19 genotype or albumin/AAG given.
- **Dosing:** sertraline 25 mg/day to start, raised in 25 mg steps to at most 100 mg for lack of response
  (maximum doses 50-100 mg, Table 1).
- **Sampling (p2):** maternal plasma around week 21 (range 17-25), around week 30 (range 26-36), the morning
  after delivery, and 1 month postpartum (the non-pregnant reference); cord blood; infant plasma at 48 h. All
  described as steady-state total plasma concentrations.
- **Analysis (p3):** concentration/dose ratio, (ng/mL)/(mg/day). Alteration ratio (AR) = pregnant C/D divided by
  the same woman's 1-month postpartum C/D, in the 8 women with a postpartum value. Medians, IQR; Wilcoxon signed
  rank. Molar to mass conversion with MW 306.2 (sertraline) and 292.2 (desmethylsertraline).

**Direction of the change:** every median AR for sertraline is below 1, so dose-adjusted concentrations were
lower in pregnancy than 1 month postpartum. The 67% in the abstract and on pp4 and 6 is the rise from the
second-trimester median (0.15) to the postpartum median (0.25); it was not significant (p = 0.345, p4). The text
(p4) also gives a median AR of 0.83 for sertraline and 0.82 for desmethylsertraline without saying which time
points these pool; Table 3 gives no 0.83. The authors conclude that concentrations seem lower in pregnancy than
postpartum, probably from increased metabolism, though not in every woman (p6).

## Pharmacodynamics and end points
- No concentration-response relation: no significant correlation between sertraline concentration and change in
  MADRS (p4; details in the supplement). The introduction quotes a therapeutic range of 10-50 ng/mL (p2).
- Neonatal outcomes (Table 1, p5): APGAR 9-10 at 5 and 10 min, no modified Finnegan NAS above 8; NAS 4 in two
  sertraline-exposed infants and 7 in one placebo infant; one transient low glucose (2.5 mmol/L); one infant born
  at 33 weeks.

## Covariates
None modelled. Variability is attributed to CYP2C19 polymorphism and to falling albumin and AAG in pregnancy (p6),
neither measured.

## Caveats
- Very small: 9 mothers, 8 with a postpartum reference, 5 pairs for each transfer ratio. None of the results is
  significant (p6).
- The non-pregnant reference is 1 month postpartum, not pre-pregnancy, and doses were titrated upward during the
  study (the median rose from 50 to 75 mg), so the comparison is not dose-matched.
- Total concentrations only; no unbound sertraline measured (p6).
- **Source typos** (Table 2 confirmed on the rendered p5): the desmethylsertraline second-trimester range is
  printed "15.78-10.52" (minimum above maximum), and the text on p4 gives the maternal desmethylsertraline at
  delivery as 33.60 ng/mL with "range 7.01-31.36" (median above the maximum), where Table 2 gives 7.01-61.36.

## Verification against stanpumpR
- **`R/drugs_sertraline.R:136`, "the same direction"**: the code applies a clearance factor of 0.60 because
  Westin found sertraline *higher* in pregnancy than at baseline. Heinonen found it *lower* in pregnancy than 1
  month postpartum: every median alteration ratio is below 1 (0.88, 0.69, 0.85; Table 3), and the authors
  conclude increased metabolism in pregnancy. That is the opposite direction, not the same. **discrepancy**
- **Registry :70, "3rd-trimester dose-adjusted concentration 0.19 vs 0.25 postpartum (67% relative difference,
  direction consistent)"**: 0.19 and 0.25 are correct medians (Table 2), but 0.25/0.19 is a 32% difference
  (derived); the 67% is the second trimester (0.15) against postpartum. "Direction consistent" is wrong for the
  same reason as above: lower in pregnancy, not higher. **discrepancy**
- **Reference entry (:167-168)**: authors, journal, volume, pages and DOI correct. **matches**
- The factor itself (0.60) rests on Westin 2017, not on this paper, and is not affected; only the claim that
  Heinonen supports it is wrong. Heinonen's result is small and non-significant, so it neither confirms nor
  refutes Westin, but it points the other way. Not a cause guessed here: whether a 1-month postpartum reference
  can sit above the pre-pregnancy level is not tested in this paper.
