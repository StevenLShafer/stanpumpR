Nitrous oxide is entered as a **fresh gas flow** in L/min at the flowmeter, like oxygen and air; its inspired concentration is its share of the total fresh gas flow, diluted by any vapour. Entering nitrous oxide adds an oxygen row at 21 per cent of the fresh gas if there is none, so that a hypoxic mixture is not the default.

Its blood:gas partition coefficient of 0.47 is low, but it is given in high concentrations (50 to 70 per cent), so the *volume* taken up in the first minutes is large. That bulk uptake shrinks the alveolar gas and concentrates whatever else is in it: the **concentration effect** on nitrous oxide itself and the **second gas effect** on a volatile agent given with it. The engine reproduces both; see [the second gas effect scenario](scenario:second-gas-effect).

Its MAC of 104 per cent (more than one atmosphere; Mapleson's 1996 meta-analysis, where GasMan gives 110) means it cannot be a sole anesthetic at sea level; at 70 per cent it contributes about 0.67 MAC equivalents at age 40, which is why it is summed into the MAC-equivalents panel with the volatile agents. Its MAC is age-adjusted like theirs. The default recovery threshold is 10 per cent; nitrous oxide washes out fast enough that the threshold matters little.

The engine's structure and parameters are GasMan®'s, except MAC, which is Mapleson's (1996); see [The inhaled-gas engine](help:models/gas-engine). **Diffusion hypoxia** at the end of a case follows from the same coupling as the concentration effect: the nitrous oxide leaving the blood dilutes the alveolar oxygen. Turning 70 per cent nitrous oxide in 6 L/min to 6 L/min of air after 30 minutes (minute ventilation 6 L/min) takes the alveolar oxygen down to 13.9 per cent two minutes later, where the same change from 30 per cent oxygen in nitrogen goes no lower than 15.3 per cent; the oxygen panel shows it. Not modelled: diffusion into closed gas spaces and the pharmacodynamics beyond MAC.
