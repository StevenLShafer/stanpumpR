The parameters and defaults of the inhaled-gas engine are GasMan®'s, and the intent for now is to give the same answers GasMan gives. The table lists twelve differences. Eleven are deliberate, confirmed by Dr Shafer in October and November 2026, and will remain; the twelfth is a GasMan option, off by default, that has no counterpart here. Anyone comparing the two side by side should know them.

## Consequences when comparing side by side

- At any patient other than the 70 kg, 170 cm, 35-year-old man, untick **Adjust weight to fat-free mass** in the Patient Profile to give the gases GasMan's linear scaling by weight.
- Enter in GasMan the **alveolar** ventilation, 70 per cent of the minute ventilation used here, and expect a small difference whenever fresh gas flow is below the minute ventilation.
- At low fresh gas flows expect the concentrations here to run higher than GasMan's, because the oxygen consumed is no longer there to dilute them.
- Set GasMan's circuit to **Ideal**. This is the largest of the differences. With GasMan left on Semi-closed, 2 per cent sevoflurane at 8 L/min gives an alveolar concentration of 0.47 per cent at one minute and 1.59 at thirty; with the ideal circuit, here and in GasMan, it is 1.09 and 1.71. (A 70 kg patient at an alveolar ventilation of 4 L/min and a cardiac output of 5 L/min, with the engine here run as GasMan runs: no dead space and no oxygen uptake. The app's own run, a minute ventilation of 5.7 L/min with 30 per cent dead space and oxygen consumed, gives 1.11 and 1.73 with the ideal circuit.)
- To compare MAC equivalents with GasMan, set the age to 40, where the age adjustment is exactly 1, and the temperature to 37 °C, and compare one agent at a time. Even then the MAC values differ (the table above): multiply GasMan's MAC equivalents by its MAC over the one here (sevoflurane 2.1/1.8, isoflurane 1.1/1.17, desflurane 6.0/6.6, nitrous oxide 110/104), or compare the tensions, which do not depend on MAC.
- Add nitrogen as an agent in GasMan, delivered at 0 per cent (or at 78 per cent of any air flow), before comparing. Without it GasMan leaves nitrogen washout out of the uptake coupling, which by itself moves alveolar sevoflurane by about 0.3 to 0.5 per cent during a wash-in.
- Leave GasMan's vapour-volume option **off**, its default. Turned on, it matters most at low flow with a high vaporizer setting: in the repository's low-flow desflurane validation scenario (8 per cent at 0.5 L/min after ten minutes at 6 per cent and 4 L/min), turning it on raises GasMan's alveolar desflurane at 60 minutes from 4.89 per cent, which the engine here reproduces when given GasMan's settings, to 5.04 (GasMan's own run, `Ideal(V)`).
- The two integrations do not agree digit for digit at any fixed step size, and neither is exact at a finite step. They converge to a common answer as the step shrinks.

## Carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is not shown as a gas, but it is accounted for. Alveolar gas holds about 5 per cent of it (carbon dioxide production over alveolar ventilation, with production at 0.8 of oxygen consumption), so the alveolar concentrations shown add up to about 95 per cent; inspired gas, which has been through the absorber, adds up to 100 (less the 1 per cent of any air in it that is not carried; see Starting nitrogen above).

All of this is the ideal circuit's, which the app runs. GasMan's semi-closed circuit is kept in the program for the comparison with GasMan and is reached only from R (`simulateGases(circuit = "semi-closed")`), where it takes no physiology GasMan lacks: no temperature but 37 °C, no altitude, no endobronchial tube, no cardiac-output slider and no body composition (Steven Shafer, 26 November 2026); GasMan has no oxygen, and this circuit carries it as a sink without the volume coupling, the oxygen consumed leaving without its volume being made up, so its alveolar total falls short (88 per cent, with the carbon dioxide 93, after an hour of 2 L/min of oxygen with 4 L/min of nitrous oxide).

Because the patient breathes in slightly more than they breathe out, the fresh gas flow that stops rebreathing is the minute ventilation plus what is being taken up, a little above the minute ventilation itself. Exactly: rebreathing stops when

```
fresh gas flow >= minute ventilation + max(total uptake, 0)
```

where the total uptake is the volume of all the gases leaving the alveoli for the blood, in L/min (negative while they come back out, as nitrogen does at first and every agent does on emergence), plus the oxygen consumed less the carbon dioxide that replaces it. In a 70 kg patient breathing oxygen and sevoflurane that excess is about 0.05 L/min, nearly all of it the oxygen; in the first minutes of an induction with 4 L/min of nitrous oxide in 6 L/min it reaches about 0.9 L/min. A fresh gas flow equal to the minute ventilation therefore leaves a little rebreathing. With sevoflurane in oxygen the inspired gas is a blend of about 99.4 per cent fresh gas and 0.6 per cent alveolar gas; in the first minutes with that much nitrous oxide the fresh share falls to about 91 per cent.

## The rebreathing rule of thumb

The usual rule of thumb is that rebreathing stops once fresh gas flow reaches minute ventilation (Feldman JM, Lampotang S, Hendrickx J. Is rebreathing prevented when FGF equals MV? APSF Newsletter, 20 October 2022). The circuit here follows it, with the threshold raised by the uptake as above; with no uptake the two are the same. The model has no circuit volume, so a change at the vaporizer reaches the patient at once; the gas already in a real circuit takes a little time to mix out, which is not clinically important.
