Target-controlled infusion (TCI) gives an intravenous drug to reach and hold a chosen concentration in the plasma or at the site of drug effect. The clinician sets the concentration, as with a vaporizer. A computer works out from a pharmacokinetic model the boluses and infusion rates needed, allowing for the drug already taken up by the tissues, and drives a pump to deliver them. It began in Bonn in 1979, went through a generation of research programs in the 1980s and 1990s, of which STANPUMP was one, and became a commercial product in 1996. stanpumpR is a descendant of that work that simulates the pump rather than driving one.

The history is told in three companion reviews published together in 2016, on which this page draws:

- Struys MMRF, De Smet T, Glen JB, Vereecke HEM, Absalom AR, Schnider TW. The history of target-controlled infusion. *Anesth Analg* 2016;122:56-69.
- Absalom AR, Glen JB, Zwart GJC, Schnider TW, Struys MMRF. Target-controlled infusion: a mature technology. *Anesth Analg* 2016;122:70-78.
- Schnider TW, Minto CF, Struys MMRF, Absalom AR. The safety of target-controlled infusions. *Anesth Analg* 2016;122:79-85.

## Before the computer

Widmark described the accumulation of a drug during a constant infusion in 1919, for a single compartment. In 1968 Krüger-Thiemer showed how to calculate the infusion rates that reach and hold a steady blood concentration of a drug described by two or more compartments, and Vaughan and Tucker applied his method to lidocaine. Every anesthetic accumulates in the tissues, so a fixed infusion rate gives a concentration that keeps rising; the calculation that corrects for this is simple in principle, but too laborious to do by hand at the bedside.

## CATIA: Bonn, 1979

Jürgen Schüttler and Helmut Schwilden gave the first target-controlled infusion in Bonn on 1 May 1979. In 1981 Schwilden published a general method for calculating the dosage scheme in linear pharmacokinetics, the foundation on which TCI was built. In 1983 Schüttler, Schwilden and Stoeckel reported their first clinical experience with **CATIA** (computer-assisted total intravenous anesthesia), the first practical TCI system: plasma-targeted etomidate at 0.3 mcg/mL and alfentanil at 0.45 mcg/mL to induce and maintain anesthesia, with adequate effect and short recovery.

CATIA used the **BET scheme**: a **B**olus of the target concentration times the central volume, to fill the central compartment; an infusion equal to the **E**limination rate, the target times the clearance; and an exponentially declining infusion to replace the drug **T**ransferred to the peripheral compartments, falling to zero as they equilibrate. BET has two limits. It can target only the plasma, and it assumes there is no drug on board, so it cannot be used to titrate up and down during an anesthetic.

In 1985 the Bonn group used CATIA to give etomidate at linearly increasing predicted concentrations while measuring its effect on the brain, the first use of TCI to measure a drug's pharmacodynamics. In 1988 they extended it to propofol and alfentanil, and showed smooth induction, stable hemodynamics, good intraoperative titration and rapid recovery. The group went on to write IVA-SIM and, in Erlangen, IVFEED, which target the plasma or the effect site for several drugs and can also drive a linearly increasing target.

## The research programs

CATIA inspired a series of research programs, written by investigators who knew one another and exchanged concepts and algorithms freely. Their authors gave them idiosyncratic names, until in 1997 they agreed on *target-controlled infusion* as the generic term (Glass, Glen, Kenny, Schüttler and Shafer, *Anesthesiology* 1997;86:1430). A few threads run through them.

- **Leiden.** Martin Ausems and Carl Hug, with support from Janssen, built TIAC to test their alfentanil kinetics under plasma-targeted infusion. Measured concentrations differed from predicted by 22 to 32 per cent between patients. The same spread appeared with boluses and manual infusions, showing that it was the biology, not the infusion method. Compared with repeated boluses, TCI gave steadier concentrations, fewer responses to stimulation and more stable hemodynamics. Frank Engbers later built portable two-pump TCI systems on an Atari Portfolio and a Psion palmtop.
- **Alabama and Duke.** Jerry Reves saw the Bonn work as a visiting professor. With J. Michael Alvis at Alabama he wrote CACI, in Pascal on an Apple II Plus driving an IMED 929 pump, to titrate fentanyl and sufentanil in cardiac surgery. At Duke, with James Jacobs, he built CACI II, with models for fentanyl, alfentanil, sufentanil, midazolam and propofol.
- **Glasgow and Bristol.** Tackley and colleagues at Bristol built a BET propofol system. Martin White and Gavin Kenny at Glasgow built an Atari-controlled propofol system whose algorithms became those of the Diprifusor.
- **Stellenbosch, Ghent, Brussels, Santiago.** Johan Coetzee and Ralph Pina wrote STELPUMP in Turbo Pascal, one of the first with a graphical interface, and gave it away; it has been used especially widely in Asia. Tom De Smet and Michel Struys wrote RUGLOOP in C++ for Windows, named, like STANPUMP and STELPUMP, for its university, and the engine of their closed-loop propofol system. Luc Barvais in Brussels wrote TOOLBOX, and a group at the University of Chile wrote AnestFusor, later sold as ezFUSOR.

## STANPUMP

Donald Stanski spent a sabbatical in Leiden working on the TIAC studies. On returning to Stanford he recruited Steven Shafer to study pharmacokinetics and TCI. Shafer and colleagues first tested the CACI device by simulation (*Anesthesiology* 1988;68:261) and found limitations. Unable to use CACI II, Shafer wrote STANPUMP (STANford PUMP) in C, to run on any MS-DOS computer and drive a range of pumps (IMED 929, BARD Chronofusor, Harvard 22, Graseby 3400 and others). It was developed in the Stanski/Shafer laboratory from 1987 through 1997.

The early versions advanced the compartments numerically, step by step (Euler's method). The final version used an exact analytical solution of the three-compartment model.

- **Exact control of the plasma.** Jacobs published an analytical solution to the three-compartment model in 1988 and the exactly correct infusion algorithm in 1990. Bailey and Shafer published a simplified exact algorithm in 1991 (*IEEE Trans Biomed Eng* 1991;38:522).
- **Effect-site targeting.** Shafer and Gregg extended it to the effect site in 1992 (*J Pharmacokinet Biopharm* 1992;20:147). Their algorithm gives the bolus that makes the effect-site concentration peak exactly at the target, with no overshoot. Struys and colleagues describe this algorithm, with Bailey and Shafer's, as the basis of all TCI systems.

STANPUMP also served as a test bed for:

- carrying **time to peak effect** rather than ke0 between models (Shafer and Varvel, 1991), so that a pharmacodynamic study done with one kinetic model could be used with another;
- a real-time Bayesian update of the pharmacokinetic parameters from observations made during drug administration;
- output files ready for pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic model fitting, drug files read from disk, multiple syringe sizes and a batch mode for research. Its library included the hypnotics, opioids, benzodiazepines, local anesthetics and muscle relaxants, and kinetics for dogs, rats and horses as well as humans.

STANPUMP was distributed freely: its source and executable code could be copied and shared by anyone, though not sold. It was used in 268 of the 559 studies that Absalom and colleagues counted, nearly half. Its principles, and its pharmacokinetic engine, were taken into later research systems and into the commercial open TCI pumps. The original C source is kept in the repository, in `Original Stanpump/Stanpump.zip`, with its documentation; see [What is in the repository](help:repository).

## STANPUMP and the look-ahead concentration

STANPUMP was written to study pharmacokinetics, not to give anesthetics. A computer-controlled infusion pump is an unusually good instrument for a pharmacokinetic study: it records exactly what it gave, second by second, and every time the target is raised it gives a fresh bolus, so the rapid distribution phase that a conventional study samples once can be sampled again and again in the same patient. The first two published studies with STANPUMP, both in *Anesthesiology* in 1990, were of this kind, and both were about which parameters a pump should be given.

- **Alfentanil.** Daniel Raemer, Alan Buschman, John Varvel, Beverly Philip, Mark Johnson, Daniel Stein and Shafer gave alfentanil by computer-controlled infusion to 29 day-surgery patients at the Brigham and Women's Hospital and 11 older inpatients at the Palo Alto Veterans Administration Hospital, using Maitre's population parameters, the most sophisticated analysis then available (*Anesthesiology* 1990;73:66-72). The measured concentrations ran about 50 per cent above the target in both groups. Simulating the same infusions with Scott and Stanski's parameters predicted that they would have done much better, and the next 11 patients, given alfentanil on those parameters, confirmed it: a median bias of 1 per cent and a median absolute error of 17 per cent. The paper introduced the median performance error (MDPE) and the median absolute performance error (MDAPE) as the measures of a pump's bias and inaccuracy, with Varvel's argument that the median tracks what the regression that produced the parameters minimised. They have been the standard measures of TCI performance since.
- **Fentanyl.** Shafer, Varvel, Natasha Aziz and Jeffrey Scott gave fentanyl by STANPUMP to 21 patients, 11 on McClain and Hug's parameters and 10 on Scott and Stanski's (*Anesthesiology* 1990;73:1091-1102). The pump ran on an Apple II, in Pascal, for the first 14 patients and on an MS-DOS computer, in C, for the last seven, advancing the compartments by Euler's method every 15 seconds; a footnote offered STANPUMP free of charge to anyone who wrote. The median absolute error was 61 per cent with McClain's parameters and 33 per cent with Scott's. The 603 arterial samples, drawn every minute after each change of target, were then fitted all at once to a single three-compartment model: the first pharmacokinetic parameters ever estimated from a computer-controlled infusion. They had a smaller central volume and a faster initial half-life than any earlier study, because the earlier studies had not sampled the first minutes so closely. Tested against the raw data of four published fentanyl studies, they predicted those studies' concentrations better than the mean parameters the studies had themselves reported. They are the fentanyl parameters in stanpumpR's library today. The paper ended with a warning that has not aged: parameters predict well at the times the study that produced them sampled, and may not outside them; these were good from one minute to ten hours and were not to be trusted beyond.

The fentanyl paper also used the pump's own equations to explain a result. McClain's parameters, from volunteers sampled for eight hours, predicted a faster fall after 400 minutes than the patients showed. Simulating a single 1000 mcg bolus under the two parameter sets, and "sampling" each curve as the other study had, showed where the two disagreed and why: in the first minutes and after 400 minutes, which McClain's study had never seen. That is simulation used to reason about the time course of a drug, and it led directly to the next idea.

### The look-ahead concentration

While running these studies Shafer saw that the program held something the studies were not asking of it. At every moment it knew the amount of drug in every compartment, because that is what it needed to compute the next infusion rate, and it could project that state forward with the same equations it used to drive the pump. So it could answer the question asked at the end of every anesthetic: if I stop now, how long until the patient wakes?

He added the **look-ahead concentration**. The user pressed F2 and typed a concentration, by preference an effect-site concentration, and at every ten-second update the program computed how long the concentration would take to fall to it if the infusion were stopped at that instant, and printed the answer above the prompts: *Effect site level of 1.50 expected in 23.4 minutes*. The manual explains: "The pump constantly calculates the time required to drop to this concentration, assuming that the infusion is terminated at the present time. This allows you to look ahead and see the amount of time required to achieve the wake up concentration if the infusion was immediately discontinued. Usually, the wake-up concentration will be the concentration desired for awakening the patient at the conclusion of the anesthetic, hence the term look ahead concentration. However, you can use this to predict the fall to any concentration you are interested in."

In the source the routine is `look_ahead()` in `STANPUMP.C`, and the variable that holds the concentration is still called `awake_concentration`. It copies the current state of the effect site, held as the amplitude of each exponential, checks whether the concentration is already below the target, checks whether it will still be above it after 500 minutes (in which case the screen reads *>500 minutes*), and otherwise finds the crossing by binary search to within two seconds. It is called from the pump's update loop, so the number on the screen was never more than ten seconds old. The revision history records a correction in November 1991 that removed a time-dependent adjustment, and another in 1996; the method itself did not change.

Shafer's explanation to the people who used the pump was this. When you give a drug you are digging yourself into a hole. The more you give, the more accumulates in the tissues, and the longer it takes to wash out. The look-ahead concentration showed you, at every moment of the anesthetic, how deep a hole you had dug. Watching the number climb through a long infusion of fentanyl, and stay put through a long infusion of alfentanil, made plain that the time to wake is not a property of the drug's half-life but of the drug *and how long it has been given*.

### Recovery curves: from the pump to models of drug offset

Shafer and his fellow John Varvel saw that the look-ahead concentration was a general idea, and that target-controlled infusion was the key to it. Pharmacokinetics are linear in dose: double the dose and every concentration doubles, so the time for the concentration to fall by a given *fraction* does not depend on what the concentration was. A TCI infusion holds the effect-site concentration constant, at whatever level, for however long it runs. Stop it, and the time for the concentration to fall by 20, 50 or 80 per cent is the same whether the target was 0.1, 1, 10 or 100 units per mL. So the question "how long until the patient wakes?" could be asked of the drug itself, with no particular patient and no particular dose, as a function of one thing: how long the infusion had run.

They wrote the paper that introduced the **recovery curve** (Shafer and Varvel, *Pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and rational opioid selection*, *Anesthesiology* 1991;74:53-63). For fentanyl, alfentanil and sufentanil, simulated in C on an 80386 with the pump's own Euler engine, they ran TCI infusions holding a constant effect-site concentration for every duration from a few minutes to ten hours, stopped each, and recorded the time for the effect-site concentration to fall by 10, 20, 30 and so on up to 90 per cent. Plotted against the duration of the infusion, the curves for each drug (their Figure 5) showed what no half-life could:

- For a 20 per cent decrease, alfentanil and sufentanil take 10 to 15 minutes however long the infusion; fentanyl takes twice as long after four hours and 45 minutes after ten.
- For a 50 per cent decrease the three are indistinguishable after infusions of half an hour or less. Beyond that fentanyl's curve rises steeply. Sufentanil recovers faster than alfentanil for infusions shorter than about eight hours, and alfentanil faster beyond that.
- For an 80 per cent decrease, sufentanil is faster for infusions shorter than about three hours, alfentanil beyond; alfentanil's curves flatten after about four hours, because its small slow compartment has filled, while fentanyl's keep climbing.

Sufentanil has longer distribution and elimination half-lives than alfentanil, yet recovers faster from infusions of ordinary length, because its large slow compartment is still filling when the infusion stops and keeps drawing drug out of the plasma. The paper explains this with three mortgages at different rates, and ends by asking investigators to examine the relationship between pharmacokinetic parameters and recovery more critically than a table of half-lives allows. It drew the curves for the effect site, because the effect site is where the effect is, and checked that drawing them for the plasma changed the numbers slightly and the conclusions not at all. It noted, too, that the curves could never be validated directly: nine curves per opioid, ten patients per point, would be 450 to 900 patients per drug. The curves were a product of simulation, and were offered as such. It is among Shafer's most cited papers.

The paper was, by the account of three colleagues and friends at Duke, too complex. Michael Hughes, Peter Glass and James Jacobs took one curve from the family, the 50 per cent decrease, computed it for the plasma rather than the effect site, extended it to six drugs (alfentanil, fentanyl, midazolam, propofol, sufentanil and thiopental), and gave it a name: the **context-sensitive half-time** (*Anesthesiology* 1992;76:334-341), the context being the duration of the infusion. Their half-times ran from one minute after a one-minute infusion to about five hours for fentanyl after a long one, against elimination half-lives of two to ten hours that predicted none of it, and their three-bucket hydraulic model explained why in a way that readers found intuitive. Shafer and Stanski's editorial on the paper, *Improving the clinical utility of anesthetic drug pharmacokinetics*, appeared beside it. The name stuck: the context-sensitive half-time is the measure of drug offset that every anesthesia textbook now teaches.

What the name did was decisive. It attached a very specific label to a very specific thing, the time for a 50 per cent decrease in plasma concentration after a TCI infusion that held the plasma constant, and a specific name is what a concept needs to travel. Without it the insight of the 1991 paper, that simulation is the way to understand the offset of drug effect, would have gone nowhere. What is not generally appreciated is that the context-sensitive half-time rests on target-controlled infusion exactly as the recovery curves do. Hughes, Glass and Jacobs simulated infusions that held the plasma concentration constant for each duration, by the bolus-elimination-transfer scheme of Schwilden, Schüttler and Stoeckel, and chose the level, 100 ng/mL, arbitrarily, "because of the linearity of the models". That is the only way to use the linearity of pharmacokinetics in dose: hold a concentration constant, and the fractional decline after stopping is a property of the drug and the duration alone. Holding the concentration steady is also what anesthesiologists try to do by hand, with a loading dose and an infusion or a series of boluses stepped down as the case goes on; the aim is a steady level of effect. Target-controlled infusion formalises that aim with an exact mathematical solution, which is why both papers are built on it, and why the concept applies to an anesthetic given by hand as well as by pump.

### Decrement times

The context-sensitive half-time had two limits. Half is one fraction among many: the decrease that matters is whatever separates the concentration maintained during the case from the concentration at which the patient wakes, breathes or leaves the recovery room, which may be 20 per cent or 80. And the plasma is not the site of drug effect; the effect site lags it, and after a bolus or a short infusion the two can part company. In 1994 Shafer and Elizabeth Youngs, another of his fellows, generalised the name (*Pharmacokinetic parameters relevant to recovery from opioids*, *Anesthesiology* 1994;81:833-842). They called the time for any given percentage decrease after a TCI infusion the **decrement time**, so that the 20 per cent, 50 per cent and 80 per cent decrement times are the three curves the 1991 paper had overlaid, and the 50 per cent decrement time is the context-sensitive half-time by another name. The word was chosen with care: *decrement* says that only the concentration is meant, where *recovery* would imply a clinical effect that the concentration is only one determinant of. The paper then asked which of the six parameters of a three-compartment model, the three volumes and the three clearances, shortens a decrement time, by raising each in turn by 5 per cent and simulating fentanyl, alfentanil and sufentanil again with STANPUMP's engine. A small central volume and a large elimination clearance always helped. The others reversed their sign with the duration of the infusion and the size of the decrease wanted: larger peripheral volumes and distribution clearances shortened the decrement times of short infusions and small decreases, and lengthened those of long infusions and large decreases, because a compartment that takes drug up during the case gives it back afterwards. The paper offered these as design rules for new drugs, and named the one then in early trials that satisfied them, remifentanil.

That paper kept to the plasma, to keep the number of variables down, and said so. The curves that matter are the effect-site decrement times, the 1991 recovery curves under their new name, and those are what stanpumpR draws.

stanpumpR's *Time until threshold* is the look-ahead concentration, three decades on. The calculation is the same: take the state of the drug at a moment, project it forward with delivery stopped, and find when it crosses the concentration at which recovery is expected. What has changed is where it is done and how often. STANPUMP computed one number for the present moment, every ten seconds, for the patient on the table. stanpumpR computes it at every point of a simulated case and draws it as a line, so that the whole shape of the hole is visible before any drug is given: flat for remifentanil, climbing through a case for fentanyl. Under a *Plasma target* or *Effect site target* row it traces a recovery curve along the infusion, for the fraction the threshold makes of the target. The threshold is set for each drug in the library, the minimum effective analgesic concentration for the opioids, and the search runs for a day rather than 500 minutes, with a root finder in place of the binary search. See [Time until threshold](help:models/recovery), and the scenarios [Context-sensitive decrement: four opioids](scenario:context-sensitive-opioids) and [Propofol induction and maintenance](scenario:propofol-induction-maintenance).

## The Diprifusor

Propofol was launched in Europe in 1986. In 1990 ICI Pharmaceuticals brought the research groups together to consider a commercial TCI system, and a symposium at the 1992 World Congress in The Hague convinced it to proceed. ICI, later Zeneca, chose not to build pumps. Instead it built the **Diprifusor** module and supplied it to pump makers, so that every pump delivered propofol in the same way. The module contained:

- the Marsh propofol model;
- White and Kenny's control algorithms, run on two processors, an exact solution on a 16-bit processor checked by an independent Euler approximation on an 8-bit processor;
- a reader for electronically tagged, prefilled Diprivan syringes, so that it could give nothing else.

The first pump containing it received the CE mark in 1996. TCI was launched commercially at the World Congress in Sydney that year, and the Diprifusor pumps from Graseby, Alaris and Fresenius, and later Terumo, followed. About 25,000 were sold, and approval came in more than 50 countries. Their two limits were that they could not target the effect site, which arrived after the design was settled, and that they would give only branded propofol.

## Open TCI pumps

Once generic propofol appeared, clinicians wanted pumps that would take any syringe, give other drugs (remifentanil above all) and target the effect site. Carefusion and Fresenius launched the first "open" TCI pumps, beginning in 2002; second-generation pumps were first approved in 2003. They carry a library of published models for propofol, remifentanil, sufentanil, alfentanil and, in some, fentanyl and midazolam, in plasma and effect-site modes. More than 36,000 were sold from 2004 to 2013. By 2015 TCI was approved or available in at least 96 countries, and Absalom and colleagues estimated that about 2.6 million patients a year in Europe, and perhaps 5 million worldwide, received a drug by TCI. Their verdict was in their title: a mature technology.

## The United States

TCI is not approved in the United States. Discussions with the Food and Drug Administration began in 1993. Zeneca submitted the Diprifusor in 1995, the review moved between the drug and device divisions, and in 2001 the FDA declared it not approvable, holding the device responsible for the difference between predicted and measured concentrations. That difference is pharmacokinetic variability, which exists however a drug is given. AstraZeneca withdrew the application in 2004, nine years after submission. In the United States TCI is possible only with research software in studies approved by an institutional review board.

Two arguments since then bear on this. Hu, Horstman and Shafer proved that the variability of concentrations under TCI is necessarily less than after a bolus (*Anesthesiology* 2005;102:639). Schnider and colleagues proposed that a TCI device should be judged, like any pump, on whether it delivers the infusion profile its model specifies for a given target and patient, which can be verified on the bench, rather than on whether the patient's concentration matches the target.

## Safety

After about 20 years and millions of patients, Schnider and colleagues searched the literature, regulators' notices and manufacturers' reports. They found seven published reports, all of syringe or pump faults except one: a clinician who selected the propofol model for a pump containing remifentanil. Choosing the wrong model, a "TCI drug swap", is the one hazard specific to TCI, and is the counterpart of a syringe swap. The field safety notices concerned:

- the James lean-body-mass formula, which fails at high body mass index, used by the Schnider propofol and Minto remifentanil models; pumps were limited to a BMI below 35 in women and 42 in men;
- two implementations of the same Schnider model that gave different amounts of propofol during induction.

No report found an adverse event caused by the TCI algorithm. They concluded that TCI is at least as safe as a constant-rate infusion, with an upper 95 per cent bound on a serious TCI-related event of less than 1 in 7 million.

## The Open TCI initiative

The name "open TCI" was used twice. The pumps above are open in that they take any syringe. The **Open TCI initiative** ([opentci.org](https://opentci.org)) is open in the other sense: it is a public home for the raw data of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies, so that models need not be the last word of the investigators who happened to collect the data.

Steven Shafer uploaded to it all of the data from the Stanford studies behind Schnider's propofol models (1998, 1999) and Minto's remifentanil model (1997), with further data from his Stanford laboratory. Other investigators contributed their studies too. The point was to put the data together in one place, in the hope that someone would come along and build better models from the whole than any one group could build from its part.

Douglas Eleveld did. His propofol model (Eleveld, Colin, Absalom and Struys, 2018) was fitted to 30 studies, 1,033 people from premature neonates to the elderly and from 0.68 to 160 kg, and his remifentanil model (Eleveld and colleagues, 2017) to a pool of earlier studies including Minto's. Both rest primarily on the same Stanford data as the Schnider and Minto models. The Eleveld models are therefore not a rival to Schnider's and Minto's but their successors: a superset of the same data, extended to the children, the elderly and the obese that the Stanford volunteers did not include, and scaled to a measure of body size, Al-Sallami's fat-free mass, that does not fail at the extremes of weight as the James lean body mass does.

That is why stanpumpR's own propofol and remifentanil models are Eleveld's, and why it can run Schnider's and Minto's beside them (the **Minto / Schnider** box under [Graph Options](help:graph-options)): to show what the pumps compute, where it agrees with Eleveld, and where it does not. See [The Minto and Schnider models](help:models/minto-schnider), and the scenario [Obesity: why the Schnider and Minto models fail at the extremes of weight](scenario:james-lean-body-mass).

## stanpumpR

stanpumpR uses very little of the original STANPUMP code. Conceptually it is identical: an open-source program that makes complex pharmacokinetic algorithms available to support patient care, teaching and research. The difference is that **the stanpumpR app does not control drug administration**. It is also in the spirit of the Open TCI initiative: it puts the published models, and the data-driven models built from pooled data, in the hands of clinicians, to bring the science of clinical pharmacology to the people who give the drugs and its benefits to their patients. It is a web-based simulator, written in R with the Shiny framework, that shows the expected concentrations from any dosing regimen.

The R package was begun by Steven Shafer in 2019. Dean Attali rebuilt it as a maintainable Shiny application with a test suite and automated deployment. In 2026 it gained an engine for the inhaled anesthetics following GasMan®, validated by Richard Epstein. The same year STANPUMP's targeting algorithm returned as two dose-table units, *Plasma target* and *Effect site target*, which simulate a TCI pump using Shafer and Gregg's method; see [Target-controlled infusion](help:tci). The same controller is offered to real pumps by [the TCI API](help:tci-api), a stateless web service that computes the next rate for a pump's host computer, while the pump keeps its own limits and decides what to run.

## GasMan

GasMan® was developed by James H. Philip at Brigham and Women's Hospital and Harvard Medical School, and has taught a generation of anesthesiologists how the inhaled agents behave: a four-compartment model of uptake and distribution with a breathing circuit in front, animated on screen. stanpumpR's inhaled-gas engine follows its structure and parameters and departs from it only deliberately; see [The inhaled-gas engine](help:models/gas-engine).
