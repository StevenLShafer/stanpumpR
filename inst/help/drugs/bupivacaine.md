### The model

Bupivacaine's disposition is from Burm and colleagues (*Clin Pharmacokinet* 1987;13:191-203), who gave a deuterium-labelled intravenous dose shortly after an epidural dose to adult surgical patients, so that intravenous disposition and epidural absorption were measured in the same people. It is a **two-compartment** model: a central volume of 33 L, a steady-state volume of 68 L, a clearance of 0.52 L/min and a terminal half-life of 143 minutes. The intercompartmental clearance was not published; it is reconstructed from those rounded means (0.32 L/min), which keeps the clearance, the steady-state volume and the terminal slope.

### Regional anesthesia (RA)

A dose in **mg RA** is a tissue injection, a nerve block or an infiltration. It enters a depot that is absorbed into the circulation by first-order kinetics, with the whole dose assumed to arrive (bioavailability 1) and no lag. The absorption rate, 0.0187/min (a half-time of 37 minutes), is **provisional**: it is the single rate that reproduces, with this disposition, the mean peak of 1.66 mcg/mL after 168.75 mg of plain 0.375% bupivacaine for combined femoral and sciatic block (Robison and colleagues, *Br J Anaesth* 1991;66:228-231). The predicted peak time, about 50 minutes, is a model output. No published study identifies bupivacaine's absorption from a nerve block against an intravenous reference.

Absorption depends on the site and the solution. In the same study the solution with epinephrine 5 mcg/mL peaked at 0.98 mcg/mL; plain bupivacaine infiltrated into the lower abdominal wall peaked at 2.23 mcg/mL after 200 mg (Martin and Neill, *Br J Anaesth* 1987;59:1425-1430). One rate cannot describe them all, and epinephrine is not an input. Epidural absorption, which Burm measured, is biphasic (a fast and a slow process), which a single first-order depot does not represent.

A dose in **mg** is intravenous, which is useful for showing what an unintended intravascular injection does.

### Effect site and typical concentrations

None. The model describes total (bound plus unbound) bupivacaine in plasma, not the nerve block, and no band is drawn: total plasma concentration alone does not predict local anesthetic systemic toxicity, which depends on the unbound fraction, how fast the concentration rises, and the patient.

### Where to be careful

Liposomal bupivacaine is a different input and is not this model. Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein rises after surgery, raising total but not unbound concentrations. A perineural catheter infusion is entered as **mg/hr RA**: a constant rate into the same tissue depot, absorbed at the same rate as a single injection; see [the continuous infusion scenario](help:scenarios/bupivacaine-perineural-infusion).
