### The model

The fentanyl parameters are a pooled analysis from the Stanford laboratory: the code lists its sources as McClain and Hug (1980), Scott and Stanski (1987), Hudson (1986), Varvel (1989) and Shafer (1990), the last being the model fitted to computer-controlled infusion data (Shafer SL, Varvel JR, Aziz N, Scott JC. *Anesthesiology* 1990;73:1091-1102). The citation returned by the model function is Scott and Stanski's 1987 study of fentanyl and alfentanil pharmacokinetics and EEG pharmacodynamics across age.

At 70 kg the volumes are 12.1, 35.7 and 224 L and the clearances 0.632, 2.8 and 1.55 L/min.

### Covariates

Weight only: volumes scale linearly and clearances allometrically to the 0.75 power. There is no age term, although Scott and Stanski found fentanyl's pharmacodynamic sensitivity to increase with age, and no sex or height term.

### Effect site

The time to peak effect is 3.7 minutes, from the Shafer and Varvel time-to-peak analysis. Fentanyl's effect site thus lags the plasma more than remifentanil's or alfentanil's, and a bolus reaches a lower fraction of its plasma peak at the effect site.

### MEAC and typical concentrations

MEAC is 0.6 ng/mL (Gourlay and colleagues reported a mean of 0.63 ng/mL in postoperative patients). The shaded band is 0.8 to 2 MEAC, 0.48 to 1.2 ng/mL. Concentrations of 2 to 5 ng/mL are typical during surgery with an inhaled agent; respiratory depression becomes clinically important above about 2 ng/mL in a patient who is not stimulated.

### Where to be careful

Fentanyl's large slow compartment (224 L at 70 kg) is what makes its context-sensitive half-time grow from minutes to hours as an infusion continues. Nothing about a single bolus predicts the behaviour of a long infusion; see [the context-sensitive decrement scenario](scenario:context-sensitive-opioids) and [Time until threshold](help:models/recovery).
