### The infusion set changes the dose

**What reaches the patient depends on the infusion set, and the difference is large.** Nitroglycerin is taken up by the plastic of many intravenous administration sets, so part of what the pump delivers never reaches the vein. **This curve assumes the whole dose arrives**, as it does through a set that does not take up the drug. Through one that does, the patient receives, and the plasma holds, much less than drawn.

How much less was measured by Kim and colleagues (*Medicine* 2018;97:e9829). They gave healthy men 20 mcg/min for 30 minutes through 100 cm sets of three materials, in a crossover. Nine were enrolled and eight analysed: the one who fainted, in his first period, with the polyolefin set, was withdrawn.

| Set material | Peak plasma concentration, against polyolefin | Area under the curve, against polyolefin |
|---|---|---|
| Polyolefin | 1 | 1 |
| PVC | 0.33 | 0.31 |
| Polyurethane | 0.21 | 0.14 |

The area ratios are those of the paper's text; its Table 3 prints 0.26 for PVC and 0.09 for polyurethane, and the paper does not say which is right. The peak ratios agree in both places.

The fall in arterial pressure was smaller with the polyurethane set; with PVC it differed from polyolefin only at the end of the infusion. One man fainted, and one had a headache, both with the polyolefin set only.

Bench studies, reviewed by Curry and Aburawi (*Biopharm Drug Dispos* 1985;6:235-280), show where the drug goes. Up to about 85% of it was lost passing through plastic infusion sets, depending on the flow; only about 10% through a glass syringe and 80 cm of high-density polyethylene tubing; filters took up to 55%, plastic bags and giving sets more than 50% each, and together as much as 80%. Glass held it all. Noonan and colleagues (*J Pharmacokinet Biopharm* 1985;13:143-157) used a set that took up less than 1%, and checked the delivered rate by assay; that is the kind of set this curve describes. Kim and colleagues cite studies of other drugs (nicardipine, diazepam) in which longer sets and slower flows took up proportionately more. So through a PVC or polyurethane set, expect a third to a seventh of the exposure drawn here (by the paper's text; a quarter to an eleventh by its Table 3), and less still at low rates through long tubing. When a dose seems to do too little, or a change of set changes the response, look at the tubing.

The delivery path can also slow the drug's arrival even when the plastic takes none up. Armstrong's patients were infused through Teflon tubing, which does not take up nitroglycerin, yet their plasma rose far more slowly than the drug's own 2-minute half-life allows (see below).

### The model

Intravenous nitroglycerin's clearance comes from Armstrong, Watts and Moffat (*Br J Clin Pharmacol* 1983;16:385-390), who infused 21 mcg/min into ten patients with congestive cardiac failure: **6.2 L/min**, in arterial plasma. The plateau it predicts at the study's rate, 3.4 ng/mL, matches the one observed.

The volume comes from how fast the drug leaves. After an infusion stops, Armstrong's group measured half-lives of 1.1 to 2.8 minutes, consistent with the 1.9 minutes of their earlier study (*Circulation* 1980;62:160-166). One compartment with that half-life has a volume of 6.2 × 1.9 / ln 2 = **17.0 L**: it reaches 90% of its plateau in about 6 minutes, and 5 minutes after a stop 16% of the plateau remains.

The same 1983 paper also reported a slow rise, a time constant of 8.1 minutes, and the model used it until 2026. In any linear model the fall after a long infusion mirrors the rise, so a slow rise and a fast fall cannot both belong to the drug. Later studies with specific assays side with the fall. Noonan and colleagues, infusing six healthy volunteers through a non-adsorbing set, measured half-lives of 1.4 to 3.2 minutes (mean 2.3) after the last infusion stopped. Sun and colleagues (*J Clin Pharmacol* 1995;35:390-397) infused isotope-labelled nitroglycerin into 18 healthy men: the plasma was at its plateau at the first sample, 15 minutes, and in the 5 minutes after the infusion stopped it fell from about 370 pg/mL (the mean of the three study arms) to about 20 to 70 (70 in the highest arm), about a twentieth to a fifth of it. Kim and colleagues (*Medicine* 2018;97:e9829) infused 20 mcg/min into healthy men (eight analysed): the plasma reached steady state in about 5 minutes, and most samples 3 to 10 minutes after the stop were below the assay's limit. The slow rise belongs to the delivery, not the drug.

### Covariates

None in the source; the parameters are those of the study's adults, scaled to fat-free mass when [the scaling switch](help:models/fat-free-mass) is on.

### Where to be careful

Nitroglycerin's plasma kinetics are notoriously erratic. In eight healthy volunteers McNiff and colleagues (*J Pharm Sci* 1981) found an apparent clearance of 0.3 to 1 L/min/kg, **three to ten times** the one drawn here, a volume of about 3 L/kg and a half-life of about 3 minutes, with two subjects' concentrations oscillating too much to analyse. Clearance followed cardiac output in rats (Fung, *Am J Cardiol* 1987;60:4H-9H, reviewing his own study; he does not show it in people, and notes that the lower clearance of heart-failure patients may reflect their higher doses), and changes with the dose and its history: in Noonan's volunteers the steady-state concentration rose more than in proportion as the rate went from 10 to 20 to 40 mcg/min, and on returning to 10 it was two to five times higher than at the first 10, the clearance falling to about 11 L/min (venous) and staying there. One compartment with one clearance cannot show this; it draws the clearance of a patient already on the drug. Above all it depends on **where the blood is drawn**. The curve is **arterial** plasma. In 20 patients with heart failure on an infusion, Armstrong and colleagues (*Circulation* 1982;66:1273-1276) measured 25.1 ng/mL in the radial artery, 29.8 in the pulmonary artery and only 7.3 in a peripheral vein: the arm removes about 60% of the drug in one pass. **A venous sample therefore reads about a third of the curve** (7.3 / 25.1 = 0.29; the 60% extraction alone would give 0.4). Clearances derived from Sun's and Kim's data in healthy men, about 10 to 15 L/min (neither paper reports a clearance or names the sampling site), roughly agree with Armstrong's arterial 6.2 once that difference is allowed for (6.2 / 0.4 = 15.5 to 6.2 / 0.29 = 21 L/min). So this is a consistent set of numbers in the patients most likely to receive the drug, not a precise one.

**A bolus is beyond the model, so none is offered**: nitroglycerin is dosed here only as an infusion, in mcg/min or mcg/kg/min. After 0.25 or 0.5 mg intravenously for uterine relaxation at caesarean delivery, David and colleagues (*Obstet Gynecol* 1998;91:119-124; *Am J Obstet Gynecol* 2000;182:955-961) measured median venous concentrations of 38 and 80 ng/mL at 1 minute, about four times what this model gives. Those samples came from the arm of the injection, and the two papers disagree on how fast the concentration fell, so they give no model, but the drug's early volume after a bolus is smaller than one compartment can show. Curry and colleagues (*Biopharm Drug Dispos* 1993;14:107-118) gave 0.1 mg intravenously to six patients at cardiac catheterisation and found a two-compartment decline in femoral venous plasma, with half-lives of 0.4 and 7.4 minutes. Their mean rate constants describe an infusion that reaches 84% of its plateau in 2 minutes and falls to 10% of it 5 minutes after a stop, as fast as this model or faster. But their printed clearance (2.1 L/min) disagrees with their own dose over area (0.45 L/min), so their numbers are not used.

The pulmonary pressures followed the measured concentration with the same time constant as its rise, so on the same delivery there is no delay to model, but no coefficients are given and no effect is drawn. Tolerance develops within a day of continuous infusion. See [the vasoactive and resuscitation drugs](help:models/vasoactive).
