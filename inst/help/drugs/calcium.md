### What is plotted

The curve is not a concentration of calcium chloride or calcium gluconate. It is the **change in venous serum ionized calcium**, in mmol/L, above the patient's own baseline: the quantity the source study measured and modelled. A patient whose ionized calcium was 1.15 mmol/L before 1 g of calcium chloride is predicted to be at about 1.15 + 0.39 = 1.54 mmol/L at the end of a 10-minute infusion. The baseline is not part of the model and is not entered, so every panel starts at zero. Total serum calcium, most of which is bound to albumin, is not represented.

Both salts raise the same ionized calcium, so they are one drug with one curve, and a patient given both gets the sum. **Every dose names its salt in the units**: `g chloride`, `mg/kg chloride`, `mg/min chloride`, or `g gluconate`, `mg/min gluconate` and so on. There is no plain `g` or `mg` for calcium, because 1 g of calcium gluconate carries a third of the calcium in 1 g of calcium chloride, and a dose that did not say which it was would be a guess.

Doses are entered as the product: grams or milligrams of calcium chloride (the dihydrate, 147 g/mol; a 10% ampoule is 1 g in 10 mL, 6.8 mmol of calcium) or of calcium gluconate (the monohydrate, 448 g/mol; a 10% ampoule is 1 g in 10 mL, 2.2 mmol of calcium). stanpumpR converts each dose to millimoles by the salt its unit names, so that dividing by the model's volumes gives mmol/L directly.

### The study

Ansari, Conti, Michel, Yarmosh, Cole and Shafer (*Anesthesiology* 2025;142:121–31) combined three trials at Stanford in which **parturients undergoing cesarean delivery** received intravenous calcium after umbilical cord clamping: a randomized bioequivalence trial of calcium chloride 0.5 g against calcium gluconate 1.5 or 2 g, and two placebo-controlled trials of 1 g of calcium chloride for uterine atony. All doses were 10-minute infusions, and venous ionized calcium was measured before the dose and at about 5, 10, 15, 30 and 60 minutes after it began (at arbitrary times to 90 minutes in the two clinical trials). Two-compartment population pharmacokinetics were fitted in NONMEM to 311 measurements from 70 parturients: a systemic clearance of 0.183 L/min (95% CI 0.067 to 0.266), central volume 10.9 L (9.27 to 12.6), intercompartmental clearance 1.25 L/min (1.03 to 1.56) and peripheral volume 16.5 L (12.5 to 24.7). The bootstrap intervals and the likelihood profiles agreed, and a one-compartment model was rejected.

The practical results are the ones a clinician needs: 1 g of calcium chloride over 10 minutes raises ionized calcium by about 0.4 mmol/L (95% CI 0.38 to 0.42), and the rise has halved about 30 minutes (23 to 40) after the infusion starts. Both are reproduced here: 0.389 mmol/L at 10 minutes, half of it at 30 minutes, 0.157 at 60 minutes.

**This is the only pharmacokinetic study of intravenous calcium in adults.** The authors note that their findings agree with the kinetics Coté and colleagues reported in burned children and dogs in 1987, and that the long-term physiological calcium kinetics of Wastney and colleagues, from 21 days of steady-state oral dosing, do not describe an acute intravenous dose.

### How the two salts are linked

The model is written in grams of calcium chloride. The bioequivalence trial randomized 34 parturients to calcium chloride 0.5 g or calcium gluconate 1.5 or 2 g. The molecular weights of the hydrated salts predict that 3.05 g of calcium gluconate carries as much calcium as 1 g of calcium chloride; the trial's first 20 patients, dosed at a 4:1 ratio from the anhydrous weights, showed the gluconate raising the ionized calcium more, and the protocol's recalibration to 1.5 g produced curves that lay on top of the 0.5 g calcium chloride curves. The population model then estimated the ratio as a parameter: **3.11 g of calcium gluconate per gram of calcium chloride** (95% CI 2.77 to 3.48).

stanpumpR applies that ratio as the paper instructs: a dose of calcium gluconate is divided by 3.11 and run through the calcium chloride model, which it does by converting the dose to millimoles at 147.01 × 3.11 = 457.2 g per mole of calcium chloride equivalent, 2% above the stoichiometric 448 g/mol. So 3.11 g of calcium gluconate plots identically to 1 g of calcium chloride, and the usual 3 g dose over 10 minutes raises the ionized calcium by 0.375 mmol/L at the end of the infusion, 96% of calcium chloride's 0.389. The ratio was estimated over 1.5 to 3 g of calcium gluconate given over 10 minutes; its confidence interval, 2.77 to 3.48, is the uncertainty in the gluconate curve relative to the chloride curve.

The study was prompted by deaths from treating hyperkalemia with 1 g of calcium gluconate in place of 1 g of calcium chloride. Here, 1 g of calcium gluconate over 10 minutes raises the ionized calcium by 0.13 mmol/L, a third of what the same gram of calcium chloride gives. The authors also point out that the calcium gluconate package insert's maximal rate of 200 mg/min is only two-thirds of the calcium chloride insert's 100 mg/min: 300 mg/min of calcium gluconate is the equivalent. Load the [calcium chloride is not calcium gluconate](scenario:calcium-salt-substitution) scenario to see the substitution.

### The population, and who the model is offered to

Every patient was a pregnant woman at term or near it: 18 to 55 years (mean 34), 55 to 100 kg (mean 80 kg, body mass index 31 kg/m²), with a serum creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL or less, no cardiac disease, no digoxin or calcium channel blocker and no magnesium infusion, and they were bleeding (median blood loss about 800 mL) while the samples were drawn. Maternal age, weight, blood loss and venous pH were each tested as covariates and none improved the fit.

stanpumpR does **not** restrict the model to parturients. It is offered for every patient the app accepts, because it is the only description of what an intravenous dose of calcium does to the ionized calcium over the first hour, and that question arises in cardiac surgery, hyperkalemia, massive transfusion and hypocalcemia in patients of every kind. Outside parturients it is an extrapolation, and the authors list the groups they did not study: men, the elderly, children and patients with renal insufficiency. Two directions of error are predictable. A patient receiving citrated blood products rapidly will have part of the dose chelated, so the rise will be smaller than plotted. A patient on a magnesium infusion was excluded from the trials, and the model says nothing about them.

### Size scaling

The source has no size covariate and found none across 55 to 100 kg. In the library's usual way (see [Fat-free mass](help:models/fat-free-mass)), the published parameters are taken to describe the 70 kg reference adult and are scaled to fat-free mass, so that a 20 kg child receives a central volume of 3.2 L rather than 10.9 L and a 20 mg/kg dose of calcium chloride raises the ionized calcium by a plausible 0.85 mmol/L instead of 0.25. This is the library's assumption, not the paper's, and it also means that a typical study parturient (80 kg, 34 years, female) with the switch on has a central volume of 9.3 L and peaks at 0.45 mmol/L rather than the paper's 0.39. Untick *Adjust weight to fat-free mass* in the Patient Profile to use the published parameters unscaled, which is how the paper reads them for everyone.

### Where to be careful

- **The first hour is what was measured.** The slower half-life of these parameters is 109 minutes, but sampling stopped at 60 to 90 minutes, and the authors say that longer sampling would almost certainly have shown a slower terminal phase and a smaller clearance. Calcium exchanges with bone and is regulated by parathyroid hormone and calcitonin, none of which the model contains. The plot is trustworthy for an hour or two; beyond that it returns to zero faster than ionized calcium really does, and it never settles at a new steady state.
- **Rate of injection.** The trials infused over 10 minutes, within the package insert's maximal rate of 100 mg/min for calcium chloride. A bolus is outside the data: the model predicts that 1 g of calcium chloride pushed over a minute peaks at 0.62 mmol/L rather than 0.39, and over two minutes at 0.55, but no one measured those peaks.
- **No effect site and no band.** The ionized calcium is the end point, and no relationship to uterine tone, contractility, blood pressure or the ECG was modelled, so there is no effect-site curve and no shaded range. The typical range of a *change* above an unknown baseline is not a meaningful target.
