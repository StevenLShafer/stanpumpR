### The model

Lithium's parameters come from Findling and colleagues (*J Clin Psychopharmacol* 2010;30:404-410), a population analysis of the first dose of immediate-release lithium carbonate, 600 or 900 mg, in 39 children and adolescents aged 7 to 17 with bipolar I disorder, sampled richly for 48 to 72 hours. Two compartments, first-order absorption after a lag, scaled by **fat-free mass**: clearance 1.79 L/h and a total volume of 56.4 L at 53 kg of fat-free mass, clearance to the power 0.75 and the volumes in proportion. The paper prints the two half-lives (2.43 h and 27.4 h) but not the central volume, so the central volume (21.5 L) and the intercompartmental clearance (3.0 L/h) were solved from the clearance, the total volume and the half-lives. Absorption has a half-life of 30 minutes after a lag of 17 minutes, and is complete.

Scaled by fat-free mass, the children's clearance is an adult's, as the authors found ("within the range reported in adults"), so the model is applied to adults through their fat-free mass. Two adult studies that the fit never saw agree with it: Westin and colleagues measured 0.53 mmol/L (0.48-0.59) in nonpregnant women on 24 mmol a day, sampled 10-16 hours after the dose, and the model gives 0.56 mmol/L 12 hours after a once-daily dose in a 65 kg woman; Clark and colleagues measured a clearance of 1.29 L/h in three women.

### What is plotted, and the dose

The curve is **serum lithium in mmol/L** (the same number as mEq/L). Doses are **milligrams of lithium carbonate**, the salt US capsules and tablets are labelled in; the drug is named for the salt so that a milligram can never be read as elemental lithium, which is a fivefold difference. 300 mg of lithium carbonate is 8.1 mmol of lithium. The oral solution (8 mEq per 5 mL) is labelled as "equivalent to the amount of lithium in 300 mg of lithium carbonate", so enter 300 mg for each 5 mL. Sustained-release products are not offered.

### The band

The band is the **AGNP consensus update 2026** (Hart and colleagues, *Pharmacopsychiatry* 2026;59:166-295, Table 5): **0.4 to 0.8 mmol/L** for maintenance in bipolar disorder, up to 1.2 mmol/L in acute mania, sampled **12 hours after the last dose**, with a laboratory alert level of 1.2 mmol/L, which is the threshold the time until threshold reads. For patients aged 60 to 79 the guideline recommends 0.4 to 0.8 mmol/L, and above 80, 0.4 to 0.7. The US label, approved earlier, still says 0.8 to 1.2 mEq/L (0.8 to 1.0 in older patients), and that toxic concentrations, 1.5 mEq/L and above, "are close to the therapeutic range".

### Renal function is not in the model

Lithium is cleared by the kidney alone, but Findling's children showed no relation between lithium clearance and creatinine clearance (which the authors did not expect), and the model has no renal term. It describes **normal renal function**. In renal impairment, dehydration or sodium depletion, and with an NSAID, an ACE inhibitor, an angiotensin receptor blocker or a thiazide, the concentration will be **higher** than the curve, sometimes much higher. Adult models with a creatinine term exist (Jermain 1991, Yukawa 1993, Alqahtani 2020) but could not be read in full.

### Pregnancy

With the Pregnant box ticked, clearance is multiplied by **1.52**. Westin and colleagues (*BMJ Open* 2017;7:e015738) found dose-adjusted serum lithium 34% lower at 34 weeks than in the same women before pregnancy (13 women, 14 pregnancies), and Clark and colleagues (*J Clin Pharmacol* 2022;62:1385-1392) measured lithium clearance 63.5% higher by the third trimester in three women, with the volume unchanged. Clearance falls back within days of delivery, which is when toxicity is most likely; the flag describes late pregnancy only.
