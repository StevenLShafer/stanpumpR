*Time until threshold* is a general modeled quantity: at each moment t, the time it would take, starting at t with delivery stopped, for the plotted concentration to fall to a drug's own **threshold**. It is drawn as a thin black line on each panel, read against the labels at the panel's right-hand edge (in minutes, hours, days or weeks, whichever suits that panel's longest time; see [Reading the plot](help:reading-the-plot)), when **Graph Options → Time until threshold** is ticked. The line is timed on the effect-site concentration for a drug whose model has one, and on the plasma concentration otherwise (see below). What crossing the threshold MEANS is specific to the drug and the threshold chosen, and is not always anesthetic recovery: for an opioid, propofol or a volatile agent it can be read that way, because the threshold is set near where the clinical effect is expected to end; for amiodarone it marks falling out of the lower edge of the chronic therapeutic window, not a return of effect; and for an antibiotic it is a different question again — if I give no more now, how long until the modeled **free** drug concentration crosses the MIC? — explained in the section on the antibiotics below, which is not itself a redosing interval or an endpoint validated against outcome.

## The thresholds

Each drug's threshold (`endCe` in the drug library) is the concentration the line is timed to, chosen per drug for what makes that panel useful: an effect-site concentration for a drug whose model has an effect site, a plasma concentration for one without (see below). The defaults are the MEAC of each opioid other than the prodrugs codeine and tramadol (the minimum effective analgesic concentration, below which analgesia is expected to become inadequate; clinical experience puts the return of spontaneous ventilation in an apneic patient near the same concentration, but that is a rule of thumb, not something the program computes: it has no model of ventilation, and the line is only the time until the effect-site concentration falls to the number set), 1 mcg/mL for propofol, 1 mcg/mL for rocuronium, and the lower end of the typical range for most other drugs. For the volatile agents it is 0.1 of the age-adjusted MAC, for the MAC-equivalents series 0.1 MAC, and for nitrous oxide 10 per cent, not age-adjusted. For the antibiotics it is the plasma concentration at which the free drug equals the MIC (see below). For amiodarone it is 1 mcg/mL, the bottom of its therapeutic window, so the line shows how long serum amiodarone would take to fall out of the window if dosing stopped. Many drugs have no default threshold, so their line stays at zero: the corticosteroids, sugammadex, glycopyrrolate, mannitol, acute intravenous amiodarone (amiodaroneIV, whose chronic window does not describe loading), the prodrugs codeine and tramadol, the metabolites that cannot be dosed directly other than desmetramadol, the local anesthetics other than lidocaine, alprazolam, clonazepam, gabapentin, pregabalin, diclofenac (both forms), meloxicam, aspirin and diamorphine, and every antidepressant, stimulant, antiemetic, antipsychotic, antiseizure, antidiabetic, anticoagulant, antihypertensive, vasoactive and illicit drug. Each drug's page gives its threshold. A threshold of zero always means "no threshold": a concentration that decays never reaches zero, so there is nothing to time. All of them can be edited under **Settings → Drug Thresholds**; the edited values travel with the URL.

## Which concentration is timed

- **A drug with an effect site** is timed on its effect-site concentration, because that is where the effect is.
- **A drug with no effect site** is timed on its plasma concentration: among them the antibiotics, the corticosteroids, sugammadex, glycopyrrolate, mannitol, gabapentin, amiodarone, desethylamiodarone and amiodaroneIV, the prodrugs codeine and tramadol, and most of the oral analgesics, antidepressants, antipsychotics and antiseizure drugs ([The effect site and ke0](help:models/effect-site)). Of these, only the antibiotics, amiodarone, zolpidem, temazepam, ketorolac and naproxen have a threshold by default.
- **Phenytoin** and the other drugs on the nonlinear Michaelis-Menten engine (GHB, exenatide, cetagliptin, linagliptin, vildagliptin) have no time until threshold at all: the whole model is simulated numerically, and the time would need a fresh nonlinear simulation from every point. **Enalapril**, on the exact transit-chain engine, has none of the exponential amplitudes the intravenous drugs' calculation uses, but the engine times it anyway, by root-finding directly on the matrix exponential (`transitChainRecovery()`); its line simply does not appear by default because enalapril ships with no default threshold set (`endCe` 0) — set one under **Settings → Drug Thresholds** to see it. Its metabolite **enalaprilat**, formed through the same engine, has no time until threshold: the engine returns the metabolite's own columns without a recovery calculation for them.

## The antibiotics: free drug at the MIC

For an antibiotic the threshold is the **MIC** (minimum inhibitory concentration) for the organism the drug is mainly given against in the perioperative setting. Only **free** (unbound) drug acts on the organism. So the line answers: **if no more is given, how long until the modeled free concentration falls below the MIC?** That is a single threshold crossing on the model's curve — it is not a measured redosing interval, and it is not the same thing as a validated PK/PD efficacy index. The MIC itself is a measured inhibitory concentration for a particular isolate; a laboratory susceptibility breakpoint is a separate, interpretive cutoff, set by a method such as EUCAST's or CLSI's from several kinds of evidence, used to classify an isolate's MIC into interpretive categories -- the breakpoint is not read directly off a free-drug concentration curve either. The dosing-interval target that is actually validated differs by drug class: the beta-lactams by time above the MIC (%fT>MIC), vancomycin by the area under the TOTAL-drug curve divided by the MIC (AUC/MIC 400-600, the ASHP/IDSA/PIDS/SIDP target for serious MRSA infections, not a target for other infections or organisms) -- this library's own vancomycin line is timed on free drug, so it approximates but is not identical to that total-drug target -- and the aminoglycosides by their peak relative to the MIC. This line shows none of those indices; it shows only when the modeled curve itself would cross the threshold if nothing more were given, which is one ingredient a clinician weighing the dosing interval might want, not a calculation of the interval.

stanpumpR does not simulate protein binding. Each antibiotic's curve shows one fixed thing, and the threshold is placed on that curve accordingly:

- **Cefazolin's** curve is **unbound** drug, because its source model is written on free concentration. Its threshold is the MIC itself.
- **The other antibiotics'** curves are **total** drug, bound plus free, which is what a laboratory reports. Their threshold is the total concentration at which the free concentration equals the MIC, using the unbound fraction measured at that low level. For a highly bound drug this is far above the MIC. Ceftriaxone, for example, is about 93 per cent bound at these levels, so free drug reaches a 1 mg/L MIC only when the total is about 15 mg/L.

The shaded band is a second marker on the same footing: free drug at the expected **MBC**, the concentration that kills, of the common pathogens each antibiotic is given against. See [Antibiotics: the MIC line and the MBC band](help:models/antibiotics).

Each antibiotic's own page gives the sources. The free fraction is for a typical adult, and binding changes with illness. When albumin is low (critical illness, malnutrition, late pregnancy) the free fraction of the albumin-bound drugs is higher, so at a given total more drug is free; but the extra free drug is also cleared faster, so the total itself falls faster than these models, fitted in healthier people, predict. Clindamycin binds alpha-1 acid glycoprotein instead, which rises after surgery and with inflammation: its free fraction is then lower, and free drug falls below the MIC sooner than the line shows. The MIC is for a susceptible isolate. Against a known isolate with a higher MIC, raise the threshold under **Settings → Drug Thresholds**, multiplying the isolate's MIC by the table's ratio of threshold to MIC.

## Oral, intramuscular and intranasal doses

"Delivery stopped" means no further doses. It does not mean the gut stops absorbing: drug already swallowed or injected has been given and will still arrive. So in the minutes after an oral dose, while the concentration is still rising towards the threshold, the line already shows the time until it will come back down, not zero. The exception is a dose with an absorption lag: until the lag has passed the engine has no state for it, so the line is blank (not zero) for those minutes after each such dose ([absorption](help:models/absorption)). Between doses, with nothing more given, the line falls one minute per minute. It reaches zero where the concentration crosses the threshold for the last time. With regular dosing (every 8 or 12 hours, say) the line jumps up at each dose and falls between them. This applies equally when a clinical event changes the kinetics part way through.

## How it is computed for the intravenous drugs

After delivery stops, the effect-site concentration is a sum of exponentials whose amplitudes are the current compartment states:

```
Ce(t) = Σ state_i × e^(-λ_i t)
```

The time until threshold is the time at which this comes down through the threshold for the **last** time, searched over the next 24 hours. A drug timed on its plasma is searched further ahead: over the next 7 days, because an antibiotic's time above the MIC often runs past a day, or over the whole length of the plot if that is longer, because a drug whose half-life is measured in weeks can take months to fall. The last crossing is the right one because the effect site lags the plasma: straight after a bolus the effect site may still be below the threshold while on its way above it, and the time that matters is when it comes back down. Zero means the concentration is already at or below the threshold and will stay there; 1440 minutes means it is still above the threshold after a day. For a drug timed on its plasma the cap is the end of its search instead: 10080 minutes (a week), or the length of the plot on a plot longer than a week.

The search does not sample the curve, so a brief rise above the threshold cannot slip between samples. It first finds every peak and trough of the curve ahead, where its slope is zero, by applying the same search to the slope; between two of them the curve only rises or only falls, so it crosses the threshold there at most once, and whether it does is read off its value at the two ends. (The slope of a sum of exponentials is again a sum of exponentials, with one term fewer once a common factor is taken out, and a sum whose coefficients, taken in order of rate, change sign only once has at most one zero, so the search always ends.) Each crossing is then located by a root finder to within 10⁻⁶ minute, and each peak and trough to within 10⁻⁹ minute. Those tolerances, and the rounding of double-precision arithmetic, are the only approximations: the time shown is within 10⁻⁶ minute of the last crossing, and a rise above the threshold is missed only if its peak is closer to the threshold than rounding can resolve, about 10⁻¹⁴ of the concentration. Until October 2026 the crossing was bracketed on a grid of times that started at three seconds and grew by about 12 per cent a step, and a rise that began and ended between two of them was missed. An audit found a propofol bolus that took the effect site 0.0005 per cent above a 1 mcg/mL threshold for 0.6 seconds, between two grid times, so the line read zero instead of 1.6 minutes.

The calculation is repeated at every time point, which is why the line can be slow to draw for long simulations with many drugs.

## How it is computed for the inhaled agents

For a gas, "stopping delivery" is defined (by Dr Shafer, for this program) as: turn off this agent's vaporizer or flowmeter, turn the fresh gas flow up so that there is no rebreathing (as is done to wake a patient), and leave ventilation as it is. Each agent is its own decision, so each panel times that agent alone; the MAC-equivalents panel times the sum with every agent turned off. The washout is simulated forward with the coupled gas engine, so the coupling between gases is kept: sevoflurane leaves faster if nitrous oxide is turned off at the same moment, because the nitrous oxide on its way out carries it along, but the sevoflurane panel shows the time for sevoflurane alone. Unlike the intravenous drugs' root-finding search, the gas calculation is not exact to rounding: it is a fourth-order Runge-Kutta search over at most 120 starting points, with the line between them interpolated, because the gas states have no closed-form amplitudes to search on directly. See [Inhaled anesthetics](help:inhaled-agents).

Two approximations remain for the gases. The forward simulation plotted on the panel is the short-step matrix exponential described in [the methods overview](help:models/overview), which holds the uptake coupling between gases fixed within each step; the *Time until threshold* line for a gas uses the separate fourth-order Runge-Kutta search described above and carries its own, different error. And with the opioid-MAC interaction on, the opioid's effect on MAC is held at its value at the moment the agents are turned off, although in truth the opioid effect would also be changing. Whether that makes the MAC time too long or too short depends on whether the opioid's effect-site concentration was still rising or already falling at that moment — there is no single direction.

## Context sensitivity

The line makes the context-sensitive half-time visible. Hughes, Glass and Jacobs showed in 1992 that the time for the concentration to halve after an infusion stops depends on how long the infusion ran: for fentanyl it grows from minutes after a brief infusion to hours after a long one, because the peripheral compartments fill and then return drug to the plasma. Remifentanil is the opposite case: Minto, Schnider and Shafer's 1997 simulations showed the time for a 50 per cent fall in its effect-site concentration staying at a few minutes after infusions of any length up to ten hours, at 20 and at 80 years. *Time until threshold* generalises this from "halve" to "fall to the threshold", and shows it continuously through the case. How that idea grew out of STANPUMP's look-ahead concentration, and why target-controlled infusion is the key to it, is told in [TCI and the offset of drug effect](help:offset). [The context-sensitive decrement scenario](scenario:context-sensitive-opioids) runs four opioids for three hours; [the propofol maintenance scenario](scenario:propofol-induction-maintenance) shows the line climbing through a propofol infusion.

## Reading it

- The line rises during an infusion and falls after a bolus has redistributed.
- A horizontal line means a steady state: stopping now or in an hour makes no difference to the recovery time.
- A line that climbs steadily through a case is the signature of a drug accumulating in slow compartments. Stop it earlier than you think.
- The line is unavailable while normalization is on, because the threshold is a concentration and the normalized axis is not.
