A dose with `PO`, `SL`, `IM`, `IN`, `SC` or `RA` in its unit is not injected into the central compartment. It is placed in an absorption depot from which it enters the central compartment by first-order kinetics, after a lag, with only a fraction of the dose arriving at all.

The one exception is a **rate** with a route word, `mg/day PO`, which [amiodarone](help:drugs/amiodarone) and oral [digoxin](help:drugs/digoxin) offer. It is a constant-rate (zero-order) oral input, the way Pollak and colleagues modelled a daily oral dose: there is no depot, absorption rate constant, lag or bioavailability, and the daily dose enters the central compartment evenly over the day, on the drug's apparent oral parameters, until the drug's next rate row, exactly as an infusion would. A second oral rate, `mg/hr PO`, is not an exception: it is the **enteral infusion** of the levodopa intestinal gels ([levodopaIntestinalGel](help:drugs/levodopaIntestinalGel), [levodopaEntacaponeIntestinalGel](help:drugs/levodopaEntacaponeIntestinalGel)), pumped into the jejunum and absorbed from the gut by the drug's own oral absorption model (through a transit compartment for the levodopa-carbidopa gel, directly for the entacapone gel), so it fills the depot at that rate rather than entering the circulation.

There are two kinds of zero-order oral input, and they differ in what the user enters. Amiodarone's and digoxin's is the **rate** above, which runs until the next rate row. [Carvedilol](help:drugs/carvedilol)'s and [valsartan](help:drugs/valsartan)'s is an ordinary amount, `mg PO`, which the engine releases at a constant rate over a **fitted duration** and then stops: Hwang and colleagues found that no first-order model described its densely sampled absorption, so the dose enters over 0.383 hours after a 0.215 hour lag; valsartan's release lasts 4.39 hours, with no lag. [Amantadine ER](help:drugs/amantadineER) releases a share of each dose this way (20.1% over 8.79 hours) beside a first-order share. A release into the gut rather than the circulation, followed by first-order absorption, is the other form ([canagliflozin](help:drugs/canagliflozin), [pramipexole ER](help:drugs/pramipexoleER)). A drug declares that release and the engine delivers it as a short infusion on the apparent oral parameters, so the dose is never multiplied by a bioavailability that the apparent parameters already contain. Overlapping releases add, which matters only for a dosing interval shorter than the release.

## The model

For each route a drug may define three parameters:

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ka | First-order absorption rate constant (1/min). The absorption half-time is ln(2)/ka. |
| bioavailability (F) | The fraction of the dose that reaches the systemic circulation |
| tlag | A delay before absorption begins (min) |

The depot empties into the central compartment at rate ka × (amount remaining), so the plasma concentration after an oral dose is a sum of the three disposition exponentials plus one more with exponent ka, and the effect site adds ke0: five exponentials in all, each still closed-form. When ka is slower than the disposition exponents, as it is for oral opioids, the rise and the peak are set by absorption rather than by distribution: the curve is "flip-flop" kinetics, and the time of peak plasma concentration is roughly the absorption half-time plus a little.

Boluses of the same drug given intravenously add to the same compartments, so oral and intravenous doses can be mixed in one simulation.

Each drug's page shows the current absorption rate, bioavailability and lag, and where each route's parameters come from. They are not all of the same standing:

- **Fitted with the intravenous model.** Clindamycin's oral parameters were fitted together with its intravenous ones, so its bioavailability is absolute.
- **Cross-study additions.** For metronidazole, hydrocortisone, methylprednisolone and dexamethasone some or all of the oral parameters (and for dexamethasone the intramuscular ones) come from studies other than the disposition model's. Methylprednisolone's absorption rate constant is provisional: its oral exposure does not depend on it, its peak does.
- **Apparent oral parameters.** Cefalexin and amiodarone were fitted to oral data alone with the bioavailability unknown, which predicts oral concentrations correctly and intravenous ones wrongly, so both are offered by mouth only; intravenous amiodarone is the separate [amiodarone IV](help:drugs/amiodaroneIV) row. Prednisone is oral only because no routine intravenous product was verified.
- **Reduced to one input.** An oral dose of prednisolone or prednisone reaches the circulation partly as each steroid, which a single input cannot carry; effective coefficients make the oral exposures exact and the shapes approximate.
- **Derived rather than fitted.** Naloxone's intranasal bioavailability and absorption rate are derived from a published model of the concentrated spray that has no absolute bioavailability, and are initial values rather than estimates.
- **Apparent oral models.** Alprazolam, clonazepam and zolpidem were fitted to oral data alone, so their volumes and clearances are apparent (divided by the unknown bioavailability, which is carried as 1) and they are offered by mouth only.
- **Absorption added to an intravenous model.** Lorazepam's oral and intramuscular routes come from a separate five-route crossover; temazepam's oral route from a separate oral study and the label's bioavailability, on a disposition fitted to intravenous data.
- **Reduced to one input, fitted to the published curve.** Buprenorphine's sublingual source has two parallel pathways (a fast burst and a slow mucosal tail) and a bioavailability that falls with dose. A single absorption rate constant and a lag (17.6 min) were fitted together to the published input at 16 mg, which brings the peak within about 5% of the published one; each dose is scaled by its own fraction absorbed (see *Saturable absorption* below). Its intranasal route, from a nine-volunteer spray study, is research only.
- **Chosen to match a peak height.** Diazepam's oral and intramuscular absorption rates were chosen so that the typical peak matches the observed mean peak; the typical curves then peak earlier (oral) and later (intramuscular) than observed. Likewise oxycodone's ka was chosen to reproduce the height of the peak of Lalovic's mean oral curve (about 30 ng/mL after 15 mg), on top of a disposition pooled from intravenous studies, rather than taken from a fitted absorption model. It then peaks at 35 minutes, earlier than Lalovic's 65.
- **Chosen to match a time of peak.** Hydromorphone's oral parameters are provisional, and its intramuscular route has no human pharmacokinetic study behind it: its bioavailability of 1 and its 30-minute peak are a judgement.

Hydromorphone's intramuscular and intranasal absorption was revised so that each route peaks at its intended time (about 20 minutes intranasal, from Coda's data, and 30 minutes intramuscular): the delay is now carried by the absorption rate constant rather than by a lag, which also keeps the time-until-threshold readout correct, since during a lag the engine has no effect-site state to count down. Gabapentin (0.31 h) and pregabalin (0.32 h) keep the lags their sources estimated, so time until threshold reads blank for those minutes after each of their doses. Clonazepam keeps the 0.369 h lag dos Santos and colleagues estimated for its tablets. Buprenorphine's sublingual route carries a 0.29 h lag, fitted with its absorption rate so that a single input matches the published peak. Zolpidem's source absorbed it through a chain of transit compartments, which delivers the dose almost as a pure delay; it is represented by a lag of 0.25 h, the mean transit time, followed by the published absorption rate. Other oral models carry the lags their sources give, among them morphine (both forms), acetaminophen, ibuprofen, diclofenac, celecoxib, aspirin, sertraline, methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine and olanzapine; each drug's page shows its lag, and wherever the drug has a threshold the time until threshold reads blank during it in the same way. A drug's time to peak effect after an oral dose is counted from the dose, lag included.

## Oral forms, pulsed release and second depots

An oral unit may name a product form (`mg PO tablet`, `liquid`, `ER`, `DR` or `XR`; see [Units](help:units)). Where the drug's model gives that form absorption of its own ([morphine](help:drugs/morphine)'s liquid, [phenytoin](help:drugs/phenytoin)'s tablet and liquid, [carbamazepine](help:drugs/carbamazepine)'s ER, [valproate](help:drugs/valproate)'s liquid and ER), its doses are absorbed with those parameters on the same disposition and effect site, and the forms add. A form the model does not distinguish is absorbed as the drug's default oral form. Two `mg PO XR` products, Concerta [methylphenidate](help:drugs/methylphenidate) and Adderall XR ([mixed amphetamine salts](help:drugs/mixedAmphetamineSalts)), are given as fixed fractions of each dose released at fixed delays (for Adderall XR, half at once and half four hours later), each fraction absorbed as the immediate-release product. [Hydrochlorothiazide](help:drugs/hydrochlorothiazide) is given in pulses too, for an entirely different reason: it is an ordinary immediate-release tablet, but the model fitted to it describes absorption with a curve that has no constant rate — the gut empties slowly at first and faster later — so the dose is split into eight fractions a few minutes apart to reproduce that curve to a third of a percent of its peak. Those fractions describe nothing about the tablet; they are arithmetic standing in for a published shape, and its page says so. [Diclofenac](help:drugs/diclofenac), [meloxicam](help:drugs/meloxicam) and [aspirin](help:drugs/aspirin) absorb each oral dose through two parallel first-order depots, each with its own lag and share of the dose (`ka_PO2` and `fraction_PO2` in the model), the oral counterpart of the slow RA depot below.

## Regional anesthesia (RA)

`RA` is a local anesthetic injected into tissue: a peripheral nerve block or a wound infiltration. The dose enters a tissue depot and is absorbed into the systemic circulation by first-order kinetics, exactly as an oral dose is absorbed from the gut, and the curve is the total (bound plus unbound) concentration in plasma. [Lidocaine](help:drugs/lidocaine), [bupivacaine](help:drugs/bupivacaine), [ropivacaine](help:drugs/ropivacaine) and [mepivacaine](help:drugs/mepivacaine) offer it, each also as an intravenous `mg`, which shows what an unintended intravascular injection does.

A drug may also declare a **slow second depot** (`ka_RA_slow` and `fraction_RA_slow` in its model): each RA dose is then split between a fast and a slow depot absorbing in parallel, each first-order, which is how absorption from tissue usually looks. [Mepivacaine](help:drugs/mepivacaine) uses it; the other local anesthetics have one depot, because no study of a peripheral block gives both rates for them against an intravenous reference.

Each drug's absorption is site- and solution-specific: it was chosen from one study of one block, with or without epinephrine, as each drug's page records, and the bioavailability is assumed to be 1. Absorption from tissue is really faster at some sites (intercostal, epidural) than others (subcutaneous, femoral and sciatic), slowed by epinephrine at some sites and not others, and often biphasic, a fast and a slow process in parallel, which a drug with one depot does not represent. Use the curves for the shape and the scale of systemic exposure, not to predict toxicity: that depends on the unbound concentration and the patient as well.

A **continuous perineural infusion** through a catheter is entered as **mg/hr RA** ([bupivacaine](help:drugs/bupivacaine), [ropivacaine](help:drugs/ropivacaine) and [lidocaine](help:drugs/lidocaine)). It runs from its row's time at that rate into the tissue depot, until the next **mg/hr RA** row for the drug (a rate of 0 stops it), and is absorbed from the depot exactly as a single injection is. At steady state the plasma concentration is the bioavailability times the rate divided by the clearance, whatever the absorption rate; absorption sets only how fast it gets there and how slowly it falls after the infusion stops. The clearance is constant in the model, so the rise in total (bound) concentration over days of infusion, as alpha-1-acid glycoprotein rises after surgery, is not represented. A continuous RA infusion cannot be combined with a clinical event that changes the PK.

## Subcutaneous injection (SC)

`SC` is a subcutaneous injection: the incretin peptides ([semaglutide](help:drugs/semaglutide), [dulaglutide](help:drugs/dulaglutide), [tirzepatide](help:drugs/tirzepatide), [liraglutide](help:drugs/liraglutide) and [liraglutide (Watson)](help:drugs/liraglutideWatson), dosed in mg; [exenatide](help:drugs/exenatide) and [lixisenatide](help:drugs/lixisenatide), dosed in mcg), the insulins, dosed in units (`U SC`, `U/kg SC`), the anticoagulants [enoxaparin](help:drugs/enoxaparin) (`IU SC`) and [fondaparinux](help:drugs/fondaparinux) (`mg SC`), and [apomorphine](help:drugs/apomorphine) (`mg SC`). The dose enters a tissue depot and is absorbed first-order, as an RA dose is; the engine carries the two routes on the same depot, so a drug offers one or the other. The weekly peptides (semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide) offer `mg SC qwk`, a dose repeated every 7 days to the end of the plot.

An insulin unit (U) is a potency unit of each preparation, not a mass, so the insulins are plotted in **mcU/mL** (microunits per mL, the same as mU/L; for human insulin 1 mcU/mL is about 6 pmol/L), and a unit of one insulin is not converted into another. A premixed insulin (human 70/30, lispro Mix75/25 and Mix50/50) is one drug whose dose is split between its two components, each absorbed from its own depot with its own rate and lag, as Tham and colleagues modelled them.

Two inputs go beyond a single first-order depot:

- **Parallel zero-order input.** In Tham's models of [insulin lispro](help:drugs/insulinLispro) and [insulin glargine](help:drugs/insulinGlargine) a fixed share of each dose (27.1% and 17.8%) enters the circulation at a constant rate over a fixed time (1.06 h and 0.612 h) beside the first-order depot. The share enters the circulation directly from the moment of injection, and the lispro lag delays only the depot, as the paper's Figure 1 and supplement define them. Watson's [liraglutide](help:drugs/liraglutideWatson) fit uses the same form: a zero-order share into the circulation over T0, beside a depot lagged by T0.
- **Sequential zero-order input**, the whole dose released into the depot at a constant rate and then absorbed first-order, is used by [canagliflozin](help:drugs/canagliflozin)'s oral release: after a lag, each tablet is released into the gut at a constant rate over 0.604 h and absorbed first-order from there.

An **insulin pump** (continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion) is entered as **U/hr SC**: a rate into the SC depot from its row's time until the drug's next U/hr SC row (0 stops it), as `mg/hr RA` is. It is offered only where the product's label permits pump use, which in the library means insulin lispro U-100 (Humalog U-100); concentrated, premixed and long-acting insulins are not. For lispro, the zero-order share of the pump's delivery is given as the zero-order input of each 2-minute portion of it, a close approximation to the ramp a continuous rate would give; the depot share is exact.

These models are concentration only. Glucose, the glucose infusion rate of a clamp, HbA1c, urinary glucose excretion and DPP-4 inhibition are separate endpoints with fits of their own, none of which is plotted; see [Antidiabetic models and their limits](help:models/antidiabetics).

## Saturable absorption

Gabapentin is absorbed by a carrier in the small intestine that saturates, so the larger the dose, the smaller the fraction absorbed. Such a drug declares the saturation, and each oral dose is scaled by its own fraction absorbed before it reaches the engine:

```
fraction absorbed = 1 - Imax × D / (ID50 + D)      D = dose in mg
```

For gabapentin, Imax is 0.906 and ID50 571 mg (Tran and colleagues, 2017): 0.69 of a 300 mg dose is absorbed, 0.54 of 600 mg and 0.39 of 1200 mg. The drug's bioavailability is then the limit for a very small dose. Once scaled, each dose is an ordinary first-order input, so doses still add, and the drug's page shows the fraction at several doses. What is not represented is saturation shared between doses: two doses entered as separate rows at the same time are each scaled by their own size, not by their sum, and absorption from doses taken close together does not compete. Buprenorphine's sublingual bioavailability falls with the dose too, and its sublingual doses are scaled by the same expression: Björnsson and colleagues reported a power law, which was fitted to this form over the 2 to 32 mg of the marketed tablets (see [buprenorphine](help:drugs/buprenorphine)). Pregabalin, a close relative of gabapentin, is about 90 per cent absorbed whatever the dose (Bockbrader and colleagues, 2010): its absorption is linear, and [the pregabalin scenario](help:scenarios/pregabalin-linear-absorption) sets the two side by side.

## Bioavailability that rises with the dose, and exposure more than proportional to it

The same per-dose scaling carries two other shapes. Sertraline's bioavailability, in Alhadab and Brundage's meta-analysis of single doses, **rises** with the dose, as 0.639 × D / (15.5 + D): 0.49 of a 50 mg dose and 0.59 of 200 mg. Paroxetine's apparent clearance in Kim and colleagues' model **falls** with the daily dose, as (D / 25)<sup>-0.363</sup>; a linear engine cannot change its clearance dose by dose, so the dose is scaled by (D / 25)<sup>0.363</sup> instead, which gives the same steady-state exposure for a once-daily dose. Its half-life is then that of 25 mg a day at every dose. Both are described on the drugs' own pages and on [Antidepressant models and their limits](help:models/antidepressants).

## What to look for

Give oxycodone 10 mg PO and turn the plasma line on. The concentration rises to a peak at about 35 minutes, and the effect site peaks at about an hour. Compare it with the sharp peak of an intravenous bolus of any opioid. [The oral oxycodone scenario](scenario:oral-oxycodone) shows two doses six hours apart.

## Limits

First-order absorption from a depot is the usual absorption model, scaled per dose where it saturates, split between two depots, released in pulses, or given a zero-order share (the subcutaneous insulins) or a zero-order release into the depot (canagliflozin, pramipexole ER) where a drug declares it, or passed through one transit compartment before the depot (levodopa, its intestinal gel, opicapone). The levodopa intestinal gels also take an enteral infusion, `mg/hr PO`, into the depot. Three drugs have shapes of their own: enalapril is absorbed through a chain of eight transit compartments (run exactly by matrix exponential), vildagliptin through one transit compartment before its depot on its saturable engine, and exenatide by zero-order then saturable subcutaneous input. The exceptions are the two zero-order inputs straight into the circulation: amiodarone's and oral digoxin's `mg/day PO`, a constant-rate input that runs until the next rate row, and carvedilol's and valsartan's fitted release duration, which spreads an ordinary `mg PO` dose evenly over a fitted time after any lag (23 minutes for carvedilol; amantadine ER releases a fifth of each dose this way). Both run as infusions on apparent oral parameters. A zero-order release is not combined with an active metabolite, several oral formulations, saturable absorption, parallel systems or Michaelis-Menten elimination, each of which rewrites the oral dose rows itself. Enterohepatic recirculation and saturation shared between overlapping doses are not modelled. An inverse Gaussian absorption model is drafted in the repository (`R/ig_absorption.R`) but is not wired in. Oral doses are invisible to Suggest Dosing and to [target-controlled infusion](help:tci), both of which treat them, as a real pump would, as unexpected additions.
