How long after the drug is stopped will the patient wake? The answer depends not on the drug's half-life but on the drug and on how long, and how, it has been given. That understanding came out of STANPUMP, the research target-controlled infusion program described in [From CATIA to stanpumpR](help:history), through a series of papers in the early 1990s, and target-controlled infusion was the key to every one of them. This page tells that story and ends at stanpumpR's [Time until threshold](help:models/recovery), which is the same idea drawn as a curve.

## STANPUMP as an instrument

STANPUMP was written to study pharmacokinetics, not to give anesthetics. A computer-controlled infusion pump is an unusually good instrument for a pharmacokinetic study: it records exactly what it gave, second by second, and every time the target is raised it gives a fresh bolus, so the rapid distribution phase that a conventional study samples once can be sampled again and again in the same patient. The first two published studies with STANPUMP, both in *Anesthesiology* in 1990, were of this kind, and both were about which parameters a pump should be given.

- **Alfentanil.** Daniel Raemer, Alan Buschman, John Varvel, Beverly Philip, Mark Johnson, Daniel Stein and Shafer gave alfentanil by STANPUMP to 29 day-surgery patients at the Brigham and Women's Hospital and 11 older inpatients at the Palo Alto Veterans Administration Hospital; Shafer wrote the software and shipped the hardware to his Harvard colleagues, so that patients could be recruited at both institutions. The pump ran on Maitre's population parameters, the most sophisticated analysis then available (*Anesthesiology* 1990;73:66-72). The measured concentrations ran about 50 per cent above the target in both groups. Simulating the same infusions with Scott and Stanski's parameters predicted that they would have done much better, and the next 11 patients, given alfentanil on those parameters, confirmed it: a median bias of 1 per cent and a median absolute error of 17 per cent. The paper introduced the median performance error (MDPE) and the median absolute performance error (MDAPE) as the measures of a pump's bias and inaccuracy, with Varvel's argument that the median tracks what the regression that produced the parameters minimised. They have been the standard measures of TCI performance since.
- **Fentanyl.** Shafer, Varvel, Natasha Aziz and Jeffrey Scott gave fentanyl by STANPUMP to 21 patients, 11 on McClain and Hug's parameters and 10 on Scott and Stanski's (*Anesthesiology* 1990;73:1091-1102). The pump ran on an Apple II, in Pascal, for the first 14 patients and on an MS-DOS computer, in C, for the last seven, advancing the compartments by Euler's method every 15 seconds; a footnote offered STANPUMP free of charge to anyone who wrote. The median absolute error was 61 per cent with McClain's parameters and 33 per cent with Scott's. The 603 arterial samples, drawn every minute after each change of target, were then fitted all at once to a single three-compartment model: the first pharmacokinetic parameters ever estimated from a computer-controlled infusion. They had a smaller central volume and a faster initial half-life than any earlier study, because the earlier studies had not sampled the first minutes so closely. Tested against the raw data of four published fentanyl studies, they predicted those studies' concentrations better than the mean parameters the studies had themselves reported. They are the fentanyl parameters in stanpumpR's library today. The paper ended with a warning that has not aged: parameters predict well at the times the study that produced them sampled, and may not outside them; these were good from one minute to ten hours and were not to be trusted beyond.

The fentanyl paper also used the pump's own equations to explain a result. McClain's parameters, from volunteers sampled for eight hours, predicted a faster fall after 400 minutes than the patients showed. Simulating a single 1000 mcg bolus under the two parameter sets, and "sampling" each curve as the other study had, showed where the two disagreed and why: in the first minutes and after 400 minutes, which McClain's study had never seen. That is simulation used to reason about the time course of a drug, and it led directly to the next idea.

## The look-ahead concentration

While running these studies Shafer saw that the program held something the studies were not asking of it. At every moment it knew the amount of drug in every compartment, because that is what it needed to compute the next infusion rate, and it could project that state forward with the same equations it used to drive the pump. So it could answer the question asked at the end of every anesthetic: if I stop now, how long until the patient wakes?

He added the **look-ahead concentration**. The user pressed F2 and typed a concentration, by preference an effect-site concentration, and at every ten-second update the program computed how long the concentration would take to fall to it if the infusion were stopped at that instant, and printed the answer above the prompts: *Effect site level of 1.50 expected in 23.4 minutes*. The manual explains: "The pump constantly calculates the time required to drop to this concentration, assuming that the infusion is terminated at the present time. This allows you to look ahead and see the amount of time required to achieve the wake up concentration if the infusion was immediately discontinued. Usually, the wake-up concentration will be the concentration desired for awakening the patient at the conclusion of the anesthetic, hence the term look ahead concentration. However, you can use this to predict the fall to any concentration you are interested in."

In the source the routine is `look_ahead()` in `STANPUMP.C`, and the variable that holds the concentration is still called `awake_concentration`. It copies the current state of the effect site, held as the amplitude of each exponential, checks whether the concentration is already below the target, checks whether it will still be above it after 500 minutes (in which case the screen reads *>500 minutes*), and otherwise finds the crossing by binary search to within two seconds. It is called from the pump's update loop, so the number on the screen was never more than ten seconds old. The revision history records a correction in November 1991 that removed a time-dependent adjustment, and another in 1996; the method itself did not change.

Shafer's explanation to the people who used the pump was this. When you give a drug you are digging yourself into a hole. The more you give, the more accumulates in the tissues, and the longer it takes to wash out. The look-ahead concentration showed you, at every moment of the anesthetic, how deep a hole you had dug. Watching the number climb through a long infusion of fentanyl, and stay put through a long infusion of alfentanil, made plain that the time to wake is not a property of the drug's half-life but of the drug *and how long it has been given*.

## Recovery curves

Shafer and his fellow John Varvel saw that the look-ahead concentration was a general idea, and that target-controlled infusion was the key to it. Pharmacokinetics are linear in dose: double the dose and every concentration doubles, so the time for the concentration to fall by a given *fraction* does not depend on what the concentration was. A TCI infusion holds the effect-site concentration constant, at whatever level, for however long it runs. Stop it, and the time for the concentration to fall by 20, 50 or 80 per cent is the same whether the target was 0.1, 1, 10 or 100 units per mL. So the question "how long until the patient wakes?" could be asked of the drug itself, with no particular patient and no particular dose, as a function of one thing: how long the infusion had run.

They wrote the paper that introduced the **recovery curve** (Shafer and Varvel, *Pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and rational opioid selection*, *Anesthesiology* 1991;74:53-63). For fentanyl, alfentanil and sufentanil, simulated in C on an 80386 with the pump's own Euler engine, they ran TCI infusions holding a constant effect-site concentration for every duration from a few minutes to ten hours, stopped each, and recorded the time for the effect-site concentration to fall by 10, 20, 30 and so on up to 90 per cent. Plotted against the duration of the infusion, the curves for each drug (their Figure 5) showed what no half-life could:

- For a 20 per cent decrease, alfentanil and sufentanil take 10 to 15 minutes however long the infusion; fentanyl takes twice as long after four hours and 45 minutes after ten.
- For a 50 per cent decrease the three are indistinguishable after infusions of half an hour or less. Beyond that fentanyl's curve rises steeply. Sufentanil recovers faster than alfentanil for infusions shorter than about eight hours, and alfentanil faster beyond that.
- For an 80 per cent decrease, sufentanil is faster for infusions shorter than about three hours, alfentanil beyond; alfentanil's curves flatten after about four hours, because its small slow compartment has filled, while fentanyl's keep climbing.

Sufentanil has longer distribution and elimination half-lives than alfentanil, yet recovers faster from infusions of ordinary length, because its large slow compartment is still filling when the infusion stops and keeps drawing drug out of the plasma. The paper explains this with three mortgages at different rates, and ends by asking investigators to examine the relationship between pharmacokinetic parameters and recovery more critically than a table of half-lives allows. It drew the curves for the effect site, because the effect site is where the effect is, and checked that drawing them for the plasma changed the numbers slightly and the conclusions not at all. It noted, too, that the curves could never be validated directly: nine curves per opioid, ten patients per point, would be 450 to 900 patients per drug. The curves were a product of simulation, and were offered as such. It is among Shafer's most cited papers.

The paper was, by the account of three colleagues and friends at Duke, too complex. Michael Hughes, Peter Glass and James Jacobs took one curve from the family, the 50 per cent decrease, computed it for the plasma rather than the effect site, extended it to six drugs (alfentanil, fentanyl, midazolam, propofol, sufentanil and thiopental), and gave it a name: the **context-sensitive half-time** (*Anesthesiology* 1992;76:334-341), the context being the duration of the infusion. Their half-times ran from one minute after a one-minute infusion to about five hours for fentanyl after a long one, against elimination half-lives of two to ten hours that predicted none of it, and their three-bucket hydraulic model explained why in a way that readers found intuitive. Shafer and Stanski's editorial on the paper, *Improving the clinical utility of anesthetic drug pharmacokinetics*, appeared beside it. The name stuck: the context-sensitive half-time is the measure of drug offset that every anesthesia textbook now teaches.

What the name did was decisive. It attached a very specific label to a very specific thing, the time for a 50 per cent decrease in plasma concentration after a TCI infusion that held the plasma constant, and a specific name is what a concept needs to travel. Without it the insight of the 1991 paper, that simulation is the way to understand the offset of drug effect, would have gone nowhere. What is not generally appreciated is that the context-sensitive half-time rests on target-controlled infusion exactly as the recovery curves do. Hughes, Glass and Jacobs simulated infusions that held the plasma concentration constant for each duration, by the bolus-elimination-transfer scheme of Schwilden, Schüttler and Stoeckel, and chose the level, 100 ng/mL, arbitrarily, "because of the linearity of the models". That is the only way to use the linearity of pharmacokinetics in dose: hold a concentration constant, and the fractional decline after stopping is a property of the drug and the duration alone. Holding the concentration steady is also what anesthesiologists try to do by hand, with a loading dose and an infusion or a series of boluses stepped down as the case goes on; the aim is a steady level of effect. Target-controlled infusion formalises that aim with an exact mathematical solution, which is why both papers are built on it, and why the concept applies to an anesthetic given by hand as well as by pump.

## Decrement times

The context-sensitive half-time had two limits. Half is one fraction among many: the decrease that matters is whatever separates the concentration maintained during the case from the concentration at which the patient wakes, breathes or leaves the recovery room, which may be 20 per cent or 80. And the plasma is not the site of drug effect; the effect site lags it, and after a bolus or a short infusion the two can part company. In 1994 Shafer and Elizabeth Youngs, another of his fellows, generalised the name (*Pharmacokinetic parameters relevant to recovery from opioids*, *Anesthesiology* 1994;81:833-842). They called the time for any given percentage decrease after a TCI infusion the **decrement time**, so that the 20 per cent, 50 per cent and 80 per cent decrement times are the three curves the 1991 paper had overlaid, and the 50 per cent decrement time is the context-sensitive half-time by another name. The word was chosen with care: *decrement* says that only the concentration is meant, where *recovery* would imply a clinical effect that the concentration is only one determinant of. The paper then asked which of the six parameters of a three-compartment model, the three volumes and the three clearances, shortens a decrement time, by raising each in turn by 5 per cent and simulating fentanyl, alfentanil and sufentanil again with STANPUMP's engine. A small central volume and a large elimination clearance always helped. The others reversed their sign with the duration of the infusion and the size of the decrease wanted: larger peripheral volumes and distribution clearances shortened the decrement times of short infusions and small decreases, and lengthened those of long infusions and large decreases, because a compartment that takes drug up during the case gives it back afterwards. The paper offered these as design rules for new drugs, and named the one then in early trials that satisfied them, remifentanil.

The paper's own simulations kept to the plasma, to keep the number of variables down, and said so. But the name it introduced is the one the 1991 recovery curves go by today: the **effect-site decrement time**, the time for the effect-site concentration to fall by a given percentage after a target-controlled infusion of a given duration, is the decrement time of Youngs and Shafer applied where the effect is. Those are the curves that matter, and those are what stanpumpR draws.

## Every case is different

All of these papers share one limitation. Each tries to capture the offset of drug effect in a static graph: a family of curves for decreases of different percentages, or one curve for a decrease of 50 per cent. A static graph assumes a case that never happens. It takes an infusion that held one concentration from start to finish and then stopped, when a real anesthetic is a bolus at induction, a higher target for the incision, a lower one once the surgeon is closing, a second drug alongside, and a clinician who wants to know, at this moment and from this history, how long until the patient wakes. The look-ahead concentration had answered that question for the patient on the table, from the drug actually given, and it is the look-ahead concentration, not the graph, that survived.

Following this work a number of companies built computer systems that track drug delivery during a case and display the predicted concentrations and effects, with their offset, on a screen in the operating room: Dräger's SmartPilot View and GE's Navigator Applications Suite among them. And most commercial TCI pumps carry a feature nearly identical to STANPUMP's look-ahead concentration, usually called the *decrement time*: the user enters a concentration, and the pump shows how long the patient would take to fall to it if the infusion were stopped now, recomputed as the case goes on.

stanpumpR has returned to the original STANPUMP formulation with *Time until threshold*. The calculation is the look-ahead concentration's: take the state of the drug at a moment, project it forward with delivery stopped, and find when it crosses the concentration at which recovery is expected. The difference is that stanpumpR does not show a single number. It does the calculation at every point of the simulated case and plots the result as a curve, read against an axis on the right of the panel: time along the bottom, and at each time how long the concentration would take to fall to the threshold if drug delivery stopped then. Nobody had seen curves like this before stanpumpR drew them, and they take some thinking.

Consider a single bolus. The time-until-threshold curve after it is a straight line, falling at exactly one minute per minute. Why? Because at the moment of the bolus there is a certain number of minutes until the threshold, which is the height of the curve there, and with no more drug given each minute that passes takes exactly one minute off that number, until the curve meets zero where the concentration meets the threshold. An infusion bends the line upward, because each minute of delivery adds more time than the minute that passed takes away; a steady state is a horizontal line, where the two balance; a fentanyl infusion is a line that keeps climbing. This is the same concept as the recovery curves of 1991, but turned so that the horizontal axis is the clock of the case rather than the duration of an idealised infusion, and so that the curve is drawn for the case as it is actually given: boluses, targets raised and lowered, a second drug beside the first. Under a *Plasma target* or *Effect site target* row it traces a recovery curve along the infusion, for the fraction the threshold makes of the target.

Because stanpumpR is available online to anyone, including an anesthesiologist in the operating room, this curve can capture the dynamic nature of clinical anesthesia in a way that a static graph never could, which is what those graphs were trying to do three decades ago. SmartPilot View and Navigator came closer, but each showed the prediction only from the present moment, as STANPUMP had: the clinician could not see it as a graph of time against time. The threshold is set for each drug in the library, the minimum effective analgesic concentration for the opioids, and the search runs for a day rather than 500 minutes, with a root finder in place of the binary search.
